Guide

Preparing a role, and writing a question

What you produce on a panel week, and on the eleven weeks you are in the room.

The packet is asymmetric

Two versions of every case go out five days ahead of the session.

The room's packet stops at the decision point and contains no outcome. The panel's packet includes what happened, why, and what followed.

So the panel is defending a real decision to a room that has not been told what it was. The questions from the room are genuine because the questioners do not know the answer, and the outcome emerges through cross-examination rather than through an announcement at the end. Do not spoil it in your opening statement.

If you are on the panel

Your job is to justify what the real people actually did, from inside the role you are sitting in, on the date they were sitting in it.

That is a harder assignment than arguing what should have been done, and it is the one you have. Hindsight makes almost every decision on the case list look obvious. A student who gets to say what they would have done gets to posture; a student who has to defend the decision as taken has to reconstruct the constraints first. You cannot defend a decision you have not understood.

The brief you receive

Your packet includes a one-page brief for your role. It states five things, and they are the five you should be able to recite without looking.

  • Your role. What you own and what you are measured on.
  • What you knew on that date. The information actually in front of you.
  • What you did not know. The blind spot, which is where the failure lives.
  • The three questions you should expect. If they surprise you in the room, you did not read this.
  • The constraint you cannot wish away. The thing that makes the obvious answer unavailable.

The failure mode to avoid

A panel that has never done this will summarise rather than argue, and four summaries of the same case is not a session. You are not presenting the facts of the case. The room has the facts. You are accounting for a choice.

Conceding a good point scores. A panel that never yields is a panel that is not listening, and stonewalling is visible from the first minute. Knowing the material well enough to grant a fair objection and then explain why you decided as you did anyway is the strongest thing you can do in the room.

Opening statement

Two minutes. State the decision you took, the constraint you were under, and the thing you were most worried about. Do not narrate the case, and do not summarise your brief.

If you are in the room

Three written questions, submitted before class, answerable from the room's packet. Scored on the eleven weeks you are not on the panel.

A question whose answer is a fact is worth nothing. A question that forces a panelist to expose an assumption is worth the grade. The test to apply before submitting: could the panelist answer this by reading aloud from the packet? If so, rewrite it.

Worth nothingWorth the grade
What useful life did the company assign to its equipment? Your auditor signed the estimate as reasonable. What would they have had to see to call it unreasonable, and did anybody look for it?
How much of revenue came from the largest customer? You describe the customer base as growing. Name the event that would cause the top two to leave in the same quarter, and say why it cannot happen.
When did the board first discuss the issue? You saw the number eleven times before you acted. What would the twelfth report have had to say that the eleventh did not?

Questions go to the instructor, not to the panel. The panel stays live rather than pre-scripted, and the instructor arrives holding the full submitted set from which to build the session's arc. The class writes the lesson plan.

Submitting sharp questions and never speaking is half the assignment. The follow-up in the room carries the rest of the weight.

The ninety minutes

The live session is spent entirely on argument, which is only possible because the asynchronous module has already carried the case narrative. Arrive knowing the story.

  • 0–10Panel opening statementsTwo minutes each
  • 10–25Breakout rooms, four per roomSharpening questions against the openings
  • 25–75Cross-examinationThe instructor calls questions from the submitted set and the chat queue
  • 75–90The reveal and synthesisWhat actually happened, and which role called it

The breakout is not filler. It is the one thing video does better than a classroom: twenty people cannot caucus in a lecture hall, and small groups produce sharper questions in fifteen minutes than a full room produces in forty.

Session mechanics

  • Panelists rename to Name — Role. The room needs to see who it is talking to.
  • Cameras on, panel and room alike. Cold call does not work against a wall of initials.
  • Questions queue in chat and the instructor calls them. This solves turn-taking better in video than it is ever solved in person.
  • Sessions are recorded. See the course policies, particularly before the week 5 pitches.

If you cannot attend your panel week

One empty seat breaks a session, so the rule is fixed rather than negotiated in the moment. The instructor covers the role for that session. The makeup is to write the role's full position paper and open the following week's class with it.

Speaker weeks

Three sessions in the semester close an act and bring an outside speaker, in weeks 5, 9, and 13. The speaker is cross-examined, not presented to, and the panel still runs. The speaker receives the case and the panel's roles in advance and is told explicitly that the room will disagree with them.

  • 0–10Panel opening statements
  • 10–30Short cross-examination
  • 30–40The speaker's own story
  • 40–80The speaker answers the panel first, then the room
  • 80–90Wrap

How panel weeks are graded

Three things, per session.

  • Whether the role was occupied rather than described.
  • Whether the position survived questioning.
  • Whether you knew the material well enough to concede a good point instead of deflecting it.
