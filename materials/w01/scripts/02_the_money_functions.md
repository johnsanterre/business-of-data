## Script

[P1] Three people in this cluster decide what things are worth, where the money comes from, and what customers pay. They sit within thirty feet of each other and they are looking at different objects. I want to introduce each with what they own, what they are measured on, and what they cannot see, and then show you the specific way the three of them can each be right and collectively produce a disaster.

[P2] Start with the controller, sometimes paired with the treasurer. This is the person responsible for what the accounts say. A company buys a large amount of equipment. The controller decides what number represents that equipment on the balance sheet a year later, and the constraint governing every choice is that it must be defensible to somebody who was not in the room.

[P3] Here is the thing that surprises people. That number is not an estimate of what the equipment is worth. It is the portion of what was paid that has not yet been charged against a year of operations. Cash left the business once, at purchase, and the accounts spread that cost across the years that get the benefit. So the controller is not trying to say what the asset would fetch and failing. They are answering a different question and answering it correctly.

[P4] How fast the cost is spread depends on a judgment about how long the equipment will be useful, and that judgment moves reported profit without changing anything physical. Two firms with identical equipment, bought on the same day from the same seller, can report results millions apart because two finance teams took different views of how long they intend to run it. Neither is mistaken. That is week two.

[P5] It is worth being precise about what the controller is measured on, because it is not accuracy. A controller is measured on whether the accounts close on time, whether they are free of restatement, and whether the auditor signs without a qualification. None of those three rewards being closer to the market price. Two of them actively reward consistency, which means a controller who revises an estimate is doing something that costs them and helps nobody they report to.

[P6] The auditor sits next to this and is weaker than most people assume. Put two numbers in front of an auditor. The purchase price can be verified in an hour, because there is a contract and a wire confirmation. The useful life cannot be verified at all, because it is a statement about the future made by the people whose reported profit it determines. So the auditor assesses whether the estimate is reasonable, which in practice means whether it falls inside the range comparable firms use. If everyone in the industry says six years, six years is reasonable by definition, whether or not six years is right. That is a real constraint on management and it is much weaker than it looks.

[P7] There is one mechanism that pulls the recorded number toward the market number, and it is worth knowing precisely because it fires so rarely. It is called impairment. At each reporting date the firm must ask whether the asset can still generate enough over its remaining life to recover what it is carried at, and if it cannot, the value is written down at once in a single charge. So the system is not blind to the market. It is patient about it, the test has a high bar, and the result is a large dated charge that is difficult to describe as anything other than an admission. Firms do not run toward it.

[P8] What the controller cannot see is whether the market agrees. Nothing in the process that produces the balance sheet number consults a buyer. A broker pricing the same equipment works from recent comparable sales and does not ask what you paid or what your accounts say, because neither is relevant to a buyer. Two processes, no mechanism forcing them to agree, and no reason to expect they will.

[P9] Next, the chief financial officer, who is not a more senior controller. The controller records what the policy says. The chief financial officer sets the policy, decides how the firm is funded and over what term, and answers for both.

[P10] The funding question is where this role lives. A firm needs money for years and can borrow it for months, or the reverse. The chief financial officer chooses the mix, and the choice looks like a cost question, because short money is cheaper than long money almost always. It is actually a survival question, because the cheap version contains an obligation to find the money again on a date, and that date is chosen by somebody else.

[P11] So a firm can own more than it owes and fail anyway, when the money is demanded on a day it cannot be produced. That is a different failure from being worth less than you owe, it kills far faster, and the cures for the two are unrelated. That is week three.

[P12] The chief financial officer is measured on the cost of capital, on hitting the quarter, and on never being the reason the firm could not pay something. Notice that the first two reward taking the cheap short money and the third punishes it, and that the third only fires once. A finance chief who terms out the funding pays a visible premium every quarter for years against a disaster that may never arrive, and is asked at each board meeting why the competitor's funding costs less. The incentives inside the firm point away from the safer structure until the year they violently do not.

[P13] The other half of the job is what comes attached to the borrowing, which is covenants. A lender does not simply want the money back. It writes tests into the agreement, computed from the borrower's own accounts, that must hold every quarter: a ratio of debt to earnings, a ratio of earnings to interest, a minimum value of the collateral. Breach one and the lender can demand repayment immediately, and immediately means what it says.

[P14] Read that again with the controller in mind. The covenants are computed from figures the controller produces, using estimates the controller chose. So a decision about how fast to spread the cost of equipment, made for accounting reasons by somebody who does not attend the lender meetings, changes how much room the firm has before a lender can call the loan. Two functions, one number, and it is on one scorecard.

[P15] What the chief financial officer cannot see is which of their exposures move together. A funding base of four hundred separate depositors looks diversified in a spreadsheet, and if all four hundred were acquired through the same channel, work in the same industry, and read the same trade newsletter, it is one exposure wearing four hundred names. The spreadsheet counts names. Nothing in it records a shared cause.

[P16] Third in this cluster, pricing. This role decides what a customer pays, and in this industry it is harder than it is almost anywhere else, for a specific structural reason.

[P17] The cost of serving one request falls, sometimes by an order of magnitude within a year, as models get smaller for the same capability and hardware gets faster. So pricing has to be set against a cost that will be materially lower by the time the contract renews. Price at today's cost and you leave a great deal on the table and invite a competitor. Price at tomorrow's cost and you are selling below cost for as long as it takes tomorrow to arrive, which is a bet on a curve rather than a plan.

[P18] There is a second problem stacked on it. Most enterprise buyers want a predictable annual number, and most of these products have a cost that scales with use. A per-seat price is what the buyer will sign and it is uncorrelated with what the customer costs to serve. So the pricing lead is choosing between a contract that sells and a contract that is safe, and heavy users are unprofitable by construction.

[P19] Pricing is measured on revenue and on the average selling price, and those two can be moved against each other, which is where the trouble starts. A discount closes a deal today and lands in the average selling price for the life of the contract, and the person who granted it has usually moved on by the time it renews. So the pricing lead spends a surprising share of their time not setting prices but defending a floor against a sales organisation whose compensation depends on getting under it.

[P20] The floor itself is a real artefact worth knowing about. Somebody computes the cost of serving a unit of the product, adds a required margin, and publishes a number below which a deal needs approval. Every part of that is contestable. Which costs count as the cost of serving, whether the research spend is allocated to the product or held centrally, and whether the hardware is charged at what it cost or what it would cost to replace. Move any one of those and the floor moves, and a floor that moves on request is not a floor.

[P21] There is a third pressure specific to this industry. A capability that is a paid product this quarter can be a free feature in somebody else's product next quarter, because a competitor with a different business model gives it away to protect something else they sell. Pricing is not only forecasting its own cost curve. It is forecasting whether a firm it does not compete with will decide that its product should be free.

[P22] What pricing cannot see is where the cost curve goes next, and it cannot see it because that depends on research results that have not happened yet, in laboratories that are not theirs. Every price they set is a forecast of somebody else's rate of progress. That is week nine.

[P23] Now the part I actually want you to take from this segment, which is what happens when these three are each doing their job well. Consider a company that buys a large fleet of specialised processors, financed with debt, to rent out as capacity.

[P24] The controller assigns the processors a useful life of six years and spreads the cost accordingly, which is defensible, sits inside the range that comparable firms use, and an auditor will sign it. The chief financial officer borrows against those processors, and the lender's covenants are written against values that the controller's schedule produces. Pricing sets the rental rate against today's cost of capacity.

[P25] Then a new generation of processors is announced and the rental rate the market will pay for the old ones falls by half. Notice what happens. Pricing has to cut prices or lose the customers, which reduces revenue. The controller's six-year life is now optimistic, and shortening it raises the annual charge and lowers the recorded value of the fleet. The recorded value of the fleet is what the covenants are computed from, so the lender's test tightens at the moment revenue falls.

[P26] Put rough figures on it so the size is visible. A fleet costing a billion dollars, spread across six years, carries an annual charge of about a hundred and sixty-seven million. Shorten the life to four years and the annual charge becomes two hundred and fifty million, which is eighty-three million dollars of reported profit gone in a year with no change to the equipment and no cash moving. And because the recorded value falls faster, the collateral behind the borrowing shrinks on the same schedule.

[P27] Now notice the trap that this creates for the controller specifically. Shortening the life is the honest response to a fleet that is ageing faster than expected. It is also the action that reduces reported profit and tightens a lender's test in the same quarter, and the person who takes it will be asked by two other functions why they did. A controller under that pressure is not being asked to lie. They are being asked whether the estimate needs revising this quarter, and the range of defensible answers is wide enough that waiting is always defensible.

[P28] Nobody made a mistake. Six years was defensible when it was chosen. Borrowing against hard assets is ordinary. Pricing against current cost is correct. The failure lives in the fact that one external event moves all three at once, in the same direction, and no single one of the three was looking at the other two. That is the seam, and it is the shape you should expect to see repeatedly.

[P29] One practical note about how these three talk to each other, because you will be asked to sit in these seats. They use the same words for different things. Value, to the controller, means the unamortised portion of a cost. To pricing it means what a customer will pay. To the chief financial officer, when a lender is on the phone, it means what the asset would fetch in a forced sale, which is a third number and usually the lowest of the three. When you hold one of these roles, be precise about which one you mean, and assume the person across the table means a different one.

[P30] The same problem applies to the word cost. To pricing, the cost of the product is what it takes to serve one more request, because that is the number a margin is computed against. To the controller, the cost is what was paid for the equipment, sitting on the balance sheet and being released into the accounts over years. To the chief financial officer, cost most often means the cost of money, which is a rate rather than an amount. Three people saying cost, three different objects, in the same meeting, without anybody noticing.

[P31] One more, and it is the one that causes real damage. Margin. Pricing computes it against the cost of serving. Finance computes it after the equipment charge lands, which pricing neither controls nor sees. The same product line comes out profitable on the first definition and loss-making on the second, and both teams report their number in good faith to the same executive in the same week.

[P32] The practical instruction that follows is small and it will serve you all semester. When a number is quoted in a case, ask which of the three questions it answers before you decide whether it is high or low. Most of the apparent contradictions in these packets are not disagreements. They are two functions answering different questions and using the same word for the answer.

[P33] The next segment is the risk cluster: the chief risk officer, communications, and trust and safety. That group has a different problem. This one holds people who can each measure their own object precisely. The next one holds people whose object resists measurement and who are held accountable for it anyway.
