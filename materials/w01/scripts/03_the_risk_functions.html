The risk functions

Segment 3 of 7 · Introduction

The narration as written, 33 paragraphs. This is the source the audio was generated from.

1This cluster has a problem the money cluster does not. A controller can produce a number and show the arithmetic behind it. Every one of the three people I am about to introduce owns something that resists being counted, and each of them is held accountable for it with the same seriousness.

2There is a second thing they share, and it is the more important one. All three are graded on the absence of an event. The chief risk officer is doing well when nothing has gone wrong. Communications is doing well when nothing is being written about you. Trust and safety is doing well when nobody has been harmed in a way that reached a journalist. A function graded on an absence has no way to demonstrate its own value, and that shapes everything about how these three behave inside a company.

3Start with the chief risk officer. This role owns the question of what could go wrong, and it owns the requirement that somebody independent has checked the thing before it ships.

4The regime is worth naming because most people who build models have never encountered it, and it is older and more developed than they expect. It comes from banking, where supervisors have required it for well over a decade, and it has three pillars. First, the people who develop and use a model are responsible for it. Second, people who did not build it validate it independently. Third, there is governance: an inventory of what the firm has deployed, documentation of what each model is for, and a named person accountable for each one.

5The word doing the work in that structure is independent, and it is harder to arrange than it sounds. Validation is only meaningful if the validator can reach a conclusion the business does not want to hear. That requires that the validator does not report to the executive whose number they are checking, that they cannot be removed by that executive, and that a negative finding actually stops something. Remove any one of the three and you have a review, not a validation.

6So what a chief risk officer is measured on, in practice, is whether a loss occurred and whether the paperwork was in order when it did. Neither rewards being right early. A risk officer who blocks a launch on a concern that never materialises has visibly cost the firm revenue and cannot prove what they prevented. A risk officer who approves a launch that goes wrong has documentation showing the process was followed. The second position is more comfortable than the first, and everybody in the seat knows it.

7What the chief risk officer cannot see is anything they lack the authority to stop. That sounds like wordplay and it is a precise description. If a team ships a change without registering it as a model, it is not in the inventory. If it is not in the inventory, it is not validated. If it is not validated, the risk function has no record that it exists, and the failure will be described afterwards as a surprise.

8This is why the definition of what counts as a model matters so much, and why it is contested. A statistical model is obviously in scope. A rules engine somebody wrote in a spreadsheet, which sets prices for thirty thousand items, is a model in every sense that matters and is registered as a spreadsheet. The scope of the regime is decided by whoever fills in the inventory, and they are usually the team being regulated by it.

9The structure has a name you will meet again in week four, the three lines of defence, and it is worth fixing now because it explains a great deal of corporate behaviour that otherwise looks like empire building. The first line is the business that owns the risk and takes it deliberately, because taking risk is what a business does. The second line is an oversight function that sets the standards the first line has to meet and checks that it did. The third line is internal audit, which does not check the risk at all. It checks whether the second line is doing its job, and it reports to the board rather than to management, which is the only reason it can say anything uncomfortable.

10Notice what that structure is designed against. It is not designed against a rogue employee. It is designed against the ordinary situation in which a business unit is under pressure to hit a number, the person who could slow it down works for the person under pressure, and everybody involved is competent and well intentioned. Every line exists because the line below it reports to somebody with a reason to want a particular answer, and the third line exists because the second one does too.

11The two ways this fails in practice are worth stating now because you will see both. Either the role sits empty, present on the organisation chart and not in the building, or it is occupied by somebody with no authority to halt anything. The second is more common and considerably more dangerous, because it produces documentation showing that risk was consulted. That is week four.

12Second in this cluster, communications. This role owns what the company says and when it says it, and most people underrate it, because from outside it looks like writing.

13It is not writing. It is a decision under time pressure about disclosure, and the decision tree has four branches. When something has gone wrong you can correct it, pause the product, withdraw it entirely, or say nothing. Each of the four is right in some circumstance and each is catastrophic in others, and the branch has to be chosen before anybody knows how bad the thing actually is.

14The constraint that makes this hard is the one people miss. Announcing a problem is itself an event. A statement that a product has been withdrawn as a precaution creates the news story that the withdrawal was meant to manage. A firm that discloses an issue on Wednesday afternoon has caused Thursday, and the alternatives were worse or illegal or both.

15There is a version of this that is worse still, because the credibility of a reassurance comes from its cost and its cost is exactly what makes it alarming. Saying the company is well capitalised is news that somebody senior thought that needed saying. Announcing a new credit facility is evidence the firm needed one. A cheap reassurance is ignored because it is cheap and an expensive one is read correctly as a signal of distress. There is often no statement available that improves the position.

16Communications is measured on coverage and on sentiment, which is to say on things produced by other people. That is an unusual position to be graded from. It means the function's own metric moves for reasons unconnected to anything it did, and it means that a quiet quarter looks identical whether the work was excellent or absent.

17There is a clock attached to this role and it is short. The first seventy-two hours after something goes wrong determine most of what follows, because that is the window in which the firm is the primary source of information about its own problem. After it, the account belongs to whoever else has been talking. So a decision that would benefit from a week of investigation has to be made in a day, on partial information, by somebody who is not in a position to evaluate the technical severity themselves.

18The authority question underneath it is the one worth learning now. Ask, of any firm, who can actually withdraw a product, and how long it takes from that person deciding to the product being gone. The answer is rarely the person who is accountable for the consequences, it usually involves several people who are not in the same time zone, and the honest number for how long it takes is longer than any of them believe. A communications plan that assumes a product can be pulled in an hour, when the mechanism takes a day, is a plan for a different company.

19What communications cannot see is whether saying it causes the event. That is not ignorance that better work would fix. It is genuinely unknowable in advance, because the counterfactual world where the statement was not made does not run. So the function makes a call under uncertainty, the outcome is observed, and the call is judged as though the outcome had been predictable. That is week five.

20Third, trust and safety. This role owns the question of who gets hurt, which is the only object in this entire course that is not denominated in money.

21The work is more concrete than the name suggests. It is deciding what the product will refuse to do, building the systems that enforce it, reviewing what got through, and handling the cases where somebody was harmed. In practice it is also the function that reads the worst material the product produces, at volume, which has consequences for the people who do it that the organisation is often slow to take seriously.

22The structural problem here is sharper than in the other two roles, and it is worth stating exactly. Every control that trust and safety adds makes the product worse on some metric that somebody else owns. A refusal is a failed request. A slower response is a worse latency number. A stricter filter reduces engagement. So the function's entire output shows up on other people's scorecards as damage, and its own contribution shows up as an absence.

23Consider a specific version. A product change increases the time people spend using it by a substantial margin, and increases the rate of a harm by a small one. Growth owns the first number and is compensated on it. Trust and safety owns the second and is not compensated on anything. Both numbers are real, both teams are reporting accurately, and there is no shared unit in which to compare them. The decision goes to whoever is more senior, which is a fact about the organisation chart rather than about the trade-off.

24There is a measurement problem inside this role that is worth understanding, because it is the reason the function argues from anecdote while everybody else argues from dashboards. Harm is rare, it is discovered late, and much of it is never reported at all, so the observed rate is a function of how hard the firm has looked. A team that invests in detection finds more harm and appears to be getting worse. A team that does not investigate has a clean number. The metric moves in the wrong direction in response to doing the job well, which is the most corrosive property a metric can have.

25The second structural feature is that the person harmed is frequently not the customer. A customer buys the product and has a relationship with the firm, a support channel, and a contract. Somebody harmed by the product may have no relationship with the firm at all, may not know the firm was involved, and has no channel through which to appear in any system the firm operates. So the interests of that person reach the room only if somebody is employed to carry them there, which is what this function is, and it is why the role is structurally weak rather than incidentally so.

26What trust and safety cannot see is the metric they are not allowed to move. This is the most precise of the twelve blind spots. The function is asked to reduce harm without reducing engagement, and when those two conflict it is told that the engagement number is not on the table. So it optimises within a constraint it did not set and cannot inspect, and afterwards is asked why it did not do more. That is week ten.

27I want to add one thing about this role that will matter in the live sessions. The harms in these cases are real, they happened to real people, and several of the cases involve minors. Reconstructing why a company made the decision it made is the assignment, and it is not the same as endorsing the decision. If you find yourself unable to argue the company's position, that usually means you have not yet understood the constraint they were under, not that the position is indefensible. Find the constraint first. You can reject it afterwards with much more force.

28Now the interaction between these three, which is where the cases live. All three are consulted before a release, and all three can raise an objection, and none of them can stop anything on their own. Their objections go up to somebody who owns a launch date.

29So a launch review looks like this. Risk says the model has not been validated against the population it will actually serve. Trust and safety says the refusal behaviour degrades under adversarial input. Communications says that if either of those becomes public after launch the story is much worse than if the launch slips. Each is correct, each is speaking about a different object, and none has a number that can be compared with the revenue forecast on the other side of the table.

30The person deciding is weighing three qualitative concerns against one quantitative one, and the quantitative one is the only one with a spreadsheet behind it. That asymmetry is not a failure of anybody's character. It is what happens when three functions own things that resist measurement and a fourth owns something that does not.

31One consequence worth carrying with you. When you read a case in this course and find that a warning was raised and ignored, do not stop at the ignoring. Ask what the warning cost the person who raised it, what evidence they could produce, and what the person receiving it would have had to give up to act. The answer is usually that the warning was expensive to make, impossible to prove, and cheap to defer, in which case the interesting question is not why it was ignored but why anybody raised it at all.

32The other consequence is about escalation. A warning that has not yet come true is indistinguishable from a warning that is wrong. Somebody who escalates and is proved wrong is visibly wrong and has cost the firm something. Somebody who stays quiet and is proved wrong shares the failure with everybody else. Three of the four outcomes point away from raising the alarm, and that is a payoff structure rather than a character defect.

33The next segment is the build cluster: the general counsel who decides what may lawfully go into the product, vendor management who decides what work leaves the firm, and strategy who decides where the firm competes. Together those three determine what the product is actually made of.
