The build functions

Segment 4 of 7 · Introduction

The narration as written, 34 paragraphs. This is the source the audio was generated from.

1These three answer one question between them, and the question is what the product is actually made of. What went into it lawfully, which parts of the work were done by people who are not employees, and why the firm is building this thing rather than something else. None of the three would describe their job that way, and all three would recognise the description.

2The general counsel comes first, specifically the part of that office concerned with inputs. This role decides what may lawfully go into the product, which in this industry means the training data, and it is a harder question than it looks because the honest answer is frequently that nobody knows.

3Here is the shape of the problem. A model is trained on a corpus of a billion documents, assembled from material that was published, in the sense of being publicly readable, and not licensed, in the sense that nobody signed anything. Whether that use is permitted turns on doctrines that were developed for a world of individual copies and human readers, and applying them to a process that ingests a billion documents to produce a set of weights is genuinely novel. Courts are working through it now, slowly, case by case, and the answers so far are partial and jurisdiction-specific.

4So the general counsel is asked a question that has no settled answer and has to produce a decision anyway, because the alternative to deciding is not shipping. What they actually produce is a risk assessment dressed as an opinion: here is the argument we would make, here is the strength of it, here is what it costs if we lose, and here is who else is doing the same thing. That last item carries more weight than a law student would expect.

5The point students get wrong most often is worth stating directly. An unsettled question is not a permissive one. It is a question whose answer arrives later, applied to conduct that has already happened, at a scale that has since become the whole business. A firm that trained on a corpus in a year when the law was unclear does not get the benefit of that year's uncertainty when the ruling lands. It gets a ruling applied to a model that is now in ten thousand enterprise deployments.

6Notice too that the exposure grows with success. A small firm using an unlicensed corpus is not worth suing. The same firm with a large revenue line built on the same corpus is worth suing, and by then the corpus is embedded in a product with customers who have their own indemnity clauses. The legal question was cheapest to resolve at the moment nobody had an incentive to raise it.

7What the general counsel is measured on is whether the firm gets sued and whether deals close, and those two pull in opposite directions. A cautious answer protects against the first and blocks the second, and the person asking for the answer usually needs it to close something this quarter. A general counsel who says no is visible; the litigation they prevented is not.

8What the general counsel cannot see is what has not been litigated yet. The entire framework for assessing risk is built from decided cases, and the questions that matter most in this industry are the ones with no decided cases behind them. So the advice is most confident where the law is settled and least useful where the exposure is largest, which is exactly backwards from what the firm needs. That is week six.

9One more thing about this role before moving on, because it changes how you should read every legal position in these cases. There is a difference between a legal opinion and a litigation position, and firms rely on the second far more than students expect. An opinion asks what a court would decide. A litigation position asks what can be argued with a straight face, how long the argument takes to resolve, and what the other side's costs are. Those produce different advice, and in a fast-moving market the second is frequently the operative one.

10That is not cynicism about lawyers. It is a description of how uncertainty gets priced. If a question takes four years to resolve, a defensible-but-uncertain position buys four years of operating, and four years is long enough for the product to become something the court is reluctant to unwind. Delay has value, everybody involved understands that, and it is one of the reasons the same unsettled question can persist for a decade.

11Second, vendor management, which decides what work leaves the firm and on what terms. This is the least glamorous role in the twelve and it is responsible for a surprising share of what goes wrong.

12The reason work leaves the firm is straightforward. Some of it is cheaper elsewhere, some of it requires headcount the firm does not want on its own books, and some of it is work the firm avoids describing in its own recruiting materials. Data labelling, content review, evaluation, and the annotation that supervised training depends on are all commonly done by people employed by somebody else, frequently in another country, at a wage set by a market the client never examines.

13The contract is where this role lives. It specifies the scope, the quality standard, the price, and the term. What it usually does not specify is anything about the conditions under which the work is performed, because the client is buying an output and the vendor is responsible for how it is produced. That division is legally clean and it is the source of the problem.

14What vendor management is measured on is cost per unit and delivery against schedule. Both are legitimate and both are satisfied by a vendor that is squeezing its own workforce, which is invisible in either number. A vendor whose costs are lower than the market average is either more efficient or paying less, and nothing in the reporting distinguishes those two, so the metric rewards the arrangement the client would be embarrassed to describe.

15What vendor management cannot see is the far end of the contract. The client sees a delivery, an invoice, and a quality score. The client does not see the shift length, the pay rate, the psychological support available to somebody reviewing distressing material eight hours a day, or the fact that the vendor has subcontracted to a fourth party. Every one of those becomes the client's problem the moment a journalist finds it, and none of them appears in any system the client operates. That is week seven.

16The pattern worth extracting is that outsourcing moves the work outside the firm and does not move the accountability. In the press coverage, in the regulatory response, and in the eventual case study, the client's name is the one that appears. A contract can allocate legal liability. It cannot allocate the part of the exposure that matters most here, which is who the public thinks is responsible.

17There is a second thing vendor management owns that has become far more consequential recently, which is dependency on a supplier the firm cannot replace. A firm buying specialised hardware from one manufacturer, or capacity from one cloud provider, has made a vendor decision that determines its cost structure, its ability to scale, and in some cases whether it can operate at all. That is the same function, the same contracting process, and a completely different order of exposure.

18The test worth applying is short. For each major supplier, ask how long it would take to replace them and what it would cost, and if the answer is longer than the notice period in the contract, the firm is not a customer of that supplier so much as a dependent of it. Most firms have never computed that number, because nothing in the procurement process asks for it.

19And there is a variant that is harder to see, where the firm buys from six vendors and feels comfortable because six is not one, and all six assemble the same component from the same manufacturer. The firm's own records show six relationships. The exposure is one, and it cannot be found from the firm's purchase orders, because the shared dependency lives one layer down in somebody else's supply chain.

20Third, strategy. This role decides where the firm competes, which sounds abstract until you notice that it determines which of the other eleven roles have a hard job.

21The central strategic question in this industry is what the firm actually owns. A model that took a great deal of money to train is not by itself a durable position, because a competitor with similar resources trains a similar one, and a smaller competitor distils something adequate for far less. So the strategy function is looking for the thing that is expensive to copy, and the honest list is short: distribution, accumulated proprietary data, switching costs inside a customer's workflow, and regulatory position.

22There is a specific move worth naming because a whole case turns on it. If your competitor sells something and you do not, you can give away a good version of it and remove their revenue while losing nothing yourself, because you were never charging for it. Releasing model weights freely, when your business is advertising or devices or cloud, is that move. It reads externally as generosity about open technology and internally it is a pricing attack on somebody else's business model.

23What strategy is measured on is market share and the growth rate, both of which are lagging indicators of decisions made two years earlier. That produces an odd position: the function most responsible for the long term is graded on numbers that move for reasons that predate its current occupant, which makes it hard to tell a good strategist from a lucky one until much later than anybody would like.

24What strategy cannot see is whether the moat is where they think it is. Firms are systematically wrong about this, and they are wrong in a consistent direction: they believe the durable advantage is the technically difficult thing they are proud of, when it is usually the boring thing they take for granted. A firm that thinks its moat is model quality, when its moat is actually a distribution agreement, will invest in the wrong thing for years and the numbers will look fine throughout. That is week eight.

25The reason firms get this wrong is worth understanding rather than just noting. A moat is only visible when it is tested, and a moat that is working is never tested, because competitors do not attack where they will lose. So the firm accumulates evidence about the parts of its position that are being attacked and none about the parts that are not, and the quiet ones are precisely the strong ones. The information available inside the firm is systematically biased toward its weaknesses.

26There is a second reason and it is about who does the telling. Strategy is briefed by the people who run each part of the business, and each of them believes the durable advantage is the thing their team built. Nobody presents a slide arguing that their own function is commoditised and the real advantage sits in a distribution agreement somebody signed six years ago. The picture assembles from a dozen accounts that each overweight the speaker.

27Now put the three together, because they interact in a way that produces one of the more common failures in this course. Strategy decides the firm must ship a capability by a date to hold a market. Meeting the date requires a corpus assembled faster than a licensing process would allow, so the general counsel is asked whether the existing corpus is defensible rather than whether a licensed one could be built. And the evaluation work needed to check the result is scoped to a vendor, because internal headcount is not available on that schedule.

28Each of those three decisions is reasonable given the previous one. The date came from a competitive judgment, the corpus question came from the date, and the outsourcing came from the schedule. Nobody chose to take a legal risk with underpaid annotators. The firm arrived there one defensible step at a time, and if you ask afterwards who decided, there is no answer, because nobody did.

29That is the second shape of failure, the seam, and this cluster produces it more reliably than the other three. Each of these roles has a clean mandate, and the space between the mandates is where the interesting decisions live and no scorecard reaches.

30One more observation about the general counsel that will help you in the live sessions. Lawyers in these cases are frequently portrayed as obstacles, and the students who hold the role tend to play it that way, saying no to things. That is a weak version of the role. A good general counsel does not say no. They say what it would cost to say yes, and they name the specific fact that would change the answer. If you hold this role on a panel, come with the price rather than the refusal.

31A similar note about vendor management. The interesting version of this role is not the person who negotiated the contract. It is the person who has to decide whether to look. A client that audits a vendor's working conditions acquires knowledge, and knowledge that the firm holds is harder to disclaim later than knowledge it never sought. There is a genuine argument, made by serious people, that the safest position is not to ask. Deciding what you want to know is a real choice with real consequences, and it is where that case actually turns.

32The last thing to carry out of this segment is about the word open, which you are going to encounter constantly and which is used to mean at least three different things. Open weights means the parameters are downloadable. Open source means the licence permits use, modification and redistribution without restriction, which many published model licences do not. Open access means you can use it through somebody's interface, which is the weakest of the three and is often described using the other two words.

33We will fix definitions for those in the live session and then hold each other to them for twelve weeks, because at least one case turns entirely on which of the three a company meant when it used the word, and on whether the ambiguity was accidental.

34The next segment is the last cluster, the functions that face outward: the board, litigation counsel, and government affairs. Those three deal with parties the firm does not control, which changes the character of the job considerably.
