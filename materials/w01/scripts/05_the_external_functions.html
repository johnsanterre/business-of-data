The external functions

Segment 5 of 7 · Introduction

The narration as written, 38 paragraphs. This is the source the audio was generated from.

1The nine roles so far all have something in common that this last three do not. Their counterparties are inside the firm. A pricing lead who disagrees with finance can walk down the corridor, and eventually somebody senior to both of them decides.

2These three deal with parties that cannot be instructed. A board can remove a chief executive and cannot be managed by one. A court sets its own schedule. A legislature passes what it passes. That difference changes the character of the work: it becomes less about deciding and more about anticipating, and the quality of the work is judged by how little the outside party surprised you.

3Start with the board, which is the most misunderstood of the twelve. Students consistently imagine it as senior management with more authority. It is not management at all, and the distinction matters enormously in at least two of our cases.

4A board owns two things. It owns the corporate structure, meaning what kind of entity this is, what it is obliged to pursue, and who holds what rights. And it owns the chief executive, meaning the decision to hire, to pay, and to remove. That second one is the whole of the board's operational power, and it is a single instrument that can only be used at full force.

5Consider what that means. A board that disagrees with a decision cannot reverse it. It can advise, it can withhold approval of things that require board approval, and it can fire the person who made it. There is no setting between disagreeing and detonating, which is why boards look passive for years and then appear to act with sudden and disproportionate violence. The instrument has one position.

6The structure half is more interesting than it sounds, because the form a company takes is a set of promises about what it will do in future, made at a moment when nobody knows what the future will demand. A firm can be constituted to pursue profit for shareholders, or with a stated purpose that is not profit, or as a nonprofit that controls a commercial subsidiary, and each arrangement is an attempt to bind a later version of the company.

7Those structures are tested when the binding starts to cost something. A commitment that was cheap to make when the firm was small and needed nothing becomes expensive when the firm needs ten billion dollars of capital and the structure will not permit it. At that point the people who made the promise and the people who need the money are frequently the same people, and the question of who the entity actually serves stops being theoretical.

8What the board is measured on is a difficult question, because in the ordinary case nobody measures it at all. Directors are evaluated by the outcome of the firm, on a long delay, and by whether anything embarrassing happened on their watch. That is a weak feedback signal, and it is one of the reasons board quality varies so much more than executive quality.

9What the board cannot see is operations, until it is too late. This is not laziness. A board meets four to six times a year, sees a pack prepared by the people whose performance it is evaluating, and has no independent staff. Everything it knows about the company comes through a filter operated by the person it is supervising.

10The consequence is worth stating precisely. A board's information is not merely incomplete, it is curated by an interested party, and the curation is entirely legitimate. Nobody prepares a board pack by including the things that make them look worst. So a board can meet its obligations, ask reasonable questions, receive accurate answers, and still be the last group to learn something that half the company knew. That is week eleven.

11There is one structural remedy worth knowing, which is that certain functions report to the board rather than to management. Internal audit is the standard example. The point of that arrangement is to create one channel of information into the boardroom that the chief executive does not control, and the health of that channel is a reasonable proxy for the health of the governance.

12Second, litigation counsel, which is a different job from the general counsel we met in the last segment. That role decides what may go into the product. This one handles what happens when somebody sues, and the two think about risk in almost opposite ways.

13The central fact about litigation is that it is a negotiation with a ruinous backstop. The overwhelming majority of disputes settle, and both parties know that from the first day. So the filing of a case is rarely an attempt to reach a judgment. It is the opening of a negotiation in which the alternative to agreeing is a process that costs both sides enormously and produces an outcome neither can predict.

14That reframes what a complaint is. A well-drafted complaint is written to be read by the defendant's board and by the defendant's customers, not only by a judge. It is designed to make the cost of not settling visible, and the specific allegations chosen are often chosen for what they will require the defendant to disclose rather than for what they will prove.

15Which brings us to discovery, the mechanism that gives this role its character. In litigation, each side can compel the other to produce documents relevant to the dispute. Internal emails, design documents, the message somebody sent at eleven at night saying they were uncomfortable with this. All of it becomes available to the other side and much of it becomes public.

16So the cost of a lawsuit is not primarily the judgment, and often not primarily the legal fees. It is that a party you are in conflict with acquires the right to read your internal correspondence and to quote the worst of it in a public filing. Firms settle cases they would probably win because the discovery is worse than the settlement.

17What litigation counsel is measured on is total cost of disputes and the avoidance of adverse precedent, and the second is subtle. A firm may prefer to pay more to settle one case than to win it, if winning would produce a published ruling that invites twenty more. Conversely, a firm may spend disproportionately to win a case that would otherwise become the template for a class of claims. The economics are about the portfolio rather than the case.

18What litigation counsel cannot see is what discovery will surface. They can guess. They can run internal searches and interview the obvious people. But an organisation of ten thousand people generates correspondence nobody has read, and the document that decides the case is frequently one that the firm's own lawyers first see when the other side quotes it. That is week twelve.

19There is a practical consequence that reaches all the way back to the risk functions. Every honest internal warning is also a future exhibit. A person who writes down that they think a launch is unsafe has created a document that will be read aloud if the launch goes wrong. That is a good thing for accountability and it is a real disincentive to writing things down, and both of those are true at once.

20Third, government affairs, which tracks which rules apply to the firm and which are coming. The name makes it sound like lobbying. Lobbying is part of it, and the larger part is a monitoring and translation function.

21The monitoring problem is genuinely hard for a reason worth understanding. A firm operating internationally is subject to rules from many jurisdictions at once, and those rules are written by bodies that do not coordinate, on different timetables, using incompatible definitions of the same terms. Whether a system is high risk, what counts as personal data, and what a provider is obliged to disclose all have different answers in different places, and the firm must satisfy all of them simultaneously.

22The translation problem is the other half. A rule is written in general language by people who are not describing your product, and somebody has to decide what it requires of a specific system in a specific deployment. That decision is made inside the firm, early, with no authority to confirm it, and everything built afterwards depends on it being right.

23There is a structural feature of regulation that will recur throughout the course. Rules are written against the conduct that caused the last problem. They are drafted after an incident, in response to it, and they describe it precisely. So the regulatory perimeter is always shaped like the previous decade, and the interesting activity is usually just outside it, in a category the drafters were not looking at.

24That is not a criticism of regulators, who cannot legislate against things that have not happened. It is a description of an unavoidable lag, and it produces a specific and repeated pattern: an activity grows outside the perimeter, has a problem, and is brought inside afterwards. Every firm operating in the gap knows they are in it.

25What government affairs is measured on is the absence of enforcement and the successful shaping of rules while they are still drafts. The second is where the real work is. Once a rule is final it is expensive to live with and nearly impossible to change, so the function's leverage is concentrated in a consultation window that most of the company does not know is open.

26What government affairs cannot see is which rule arrives next. They can see what is being drafted, which is a different thing, because the rules that matter most arrive fast in response to an event nobody predicted. A function that plans against the published pipeline is prepared for the ordinary case and unprepared for the one that will actually reshape the industry. That is week thirteen.

27There is one more feature of this role that recurs, and it is the reason firms invest in it long before any rule applies to them. A regulator writing a rule needs to understand an industry it does not work in, and the people who understand it best work for the firms being regulated. So the drafting process depends on submissions from the regulated, which is a genuine source of expertise and an obvious conflict at the same time, and both descriptions are accurate.

28The practical consequence for a firm is that the cost of being absent from that process is high and invisible. A rule drafted without your product in mind may be impossible for your product to comply with, not because anybody intended that but because nobody in the room had a reason to raise it. Government affairs is frequently arguing about a definition rather than a principle, and the definitions are where the compliance cost lives.

29Now the interaction between these three, which produces a failure shape the other clusters do not.

30Consider a firm that discovers an internal problem. Litigation counsel notes that documenting it creates discoverable material. Government affairs notes that disclosing it to one regulator may trigger obligations in three other jurisdictions. The board has not been told, because the executives are still establishing the facts and do not want to alarm directors prematurely, which is a defensible position for about a week and indefensible after a month.

31Every one of those considerations is legitimate. Together they produce a firm that knows something, has excellent reasons not to write it down, has excellent reasons not to tell its regulator yet, and has not told its board. And the longer that state persists the more expensive disclosure becomes, because the question stops being what happened and becomes why you did not say so earlier.

32This is the third shape of failure and the one this cluster specialises in. The act of reporting the problem is what triggers the collapse. Telling the truth on Wednesday afternoon is what makes Thursday happen, and by the time it is obvious that Wednesday is necessary, the alternatives are worse or illegal or both.

33It is worth separating two things that get confused here, because a panel will collapse them. There is a version of this where a firm conceals a problem to avoid consequences, which is straightforwardly wrong and occasionally criminal. And there is a version where a firm delays disclosure while it establishes facts, on advice, for reasons every adviser would endorse, and the delay itself becomes the thing it is judged for. Most of the cases in this course are the second, and the second is harder to argue about, which is why it is worth the ninety minutes.

34The test that separates them is usually about who benefits from the delay. If the extra week is spent verifying the scope of the problem, that is investigation. If it is spent on whether disclosure can be avoided, or timed to a quieter news cycle, or scoped so narrowly that the disclosure is technically accurate and materially misleading, that is something else. The same week looks identical from outside, which is why the internal correspondence matters so much, and why it is the first thing discovery asks for.

35I want to be careful about how that lands, because it is easy to hear as an argument for concealment and it is the opposite. The pattern exists because disclosure gets more expensive with time, which means the cheapest moment to disclose is always now and it never feels like it. A firm that understands the shape discloses early precisely because it knows the curve. A firm that does not, discovers the curve on the day it can no longer afford it.

36One last observation, which connects this cluster back to the first segment. Each of these three roles is an interface between the firm and a party that will eventually judge it: the owners, the courts, and the state. None of the three has authority over that party, and all three are held responsible for its behaviour. That is a strange position to occupy, and it explains why these functions are unusually well informed and unusually cautious.

37So that is the map. Twelve roles, four clusters, and for each one what they own, what they are measured on, and what they cannot see. The reference sheet with all twelve is a reading for this week and it is the document worth keeping open every week after.

38The remaining two segments are about the mechanics rather than the people. First, how a capability actually becomes a product, which gates it passes and who can stop it at each one. Then the three shapes of failure, taken properly, with examples from outside this industry so that the pattern is visible without the technology getting in the way.
