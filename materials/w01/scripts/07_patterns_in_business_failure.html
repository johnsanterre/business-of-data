Patterns in business failure

Segment 7 of 7 · Introduction

The narration as written, 28 paragraphs. This is the source the audio was generated from.

1Here is the thesis of this course, and it is worth stating before the examples rather than after. Business failures are almost never one bad decision. They are a decision made correctly inside one role's frame, which turns out to be catastrophic in another's, with nobody owning the seam between them.

2That is not a comfortable claim, because it removes the villain. It is much more satisfying to read a case and conclude that somebody was reckless or stupid, and occasionally somebody was. But in the overwhelming majority of these situations every individual decision was defensible when it was taken, by a competent person, with the information they had. If you finish a case still believing somebody was an idiot, you have probably not found their constraint.

3The failures take three shapes. I am going to give each one an example from outside this industry, because you have no prior commitments about banking, aviation, or manufacturing, and the pattern is easier to see when the technology is not available to argue about.

4The first shape is the unfilled role. A function that should exist either does not exist in the building, or it exists and is occupied by somebody with no authority to stop anything. The second version is worse, because it produces documentation showing the function was consulted.

5The banking example. A trading desk generates a large share of a bank's profit. There is a risk function responsible for setting limits on what the desk may hold and for verifying how the desk values its own positions. On the organisation chart the risk function is independent. In practice the person doing it reports to somebody who reports to the head of the division that contains the desk, their bonus is a function of divisional profit, and the desk head is the most powerful person in the building.

6Now ask what happens when that risk officer disagrees with the desk's valuation of an illiquid position. They can raise it. Raising it puts them in conflict with the person who influences their compensation, over a position that is difficult to value precisely, on which reasonable people differ. The desk's number is within a defensible range. The risk officer's concern is that it is at the wrong end of the range.

7There is no version of that conversation where the risk officer wins on the first attempt, and there are several where they lose their standing to raise the second one. So the position is marked at the favourable end of the range, quarter after quarter, entirely within the rules, until the market moves and the accumulated difference between the favourable end and reality becomes a number that ends careers.

8Notice what is not in that story. Nobody lied. No limit was breached. There was a risk function, it was staffed, it reviewed the positions, and it produced reports. The role was occupied and it was not filled, and the difference between those two words is the entire lesson.

9The diagnostic question for this shape is short and you should ask it of every case. Not whether the function exists, but what happens to the person in it when they say no. If the honest answer is that they are overruled, sidelined, or replaced, then the function is decorative regardless of what the organisation chart shows.

10The second shape is the seam. Two functions, each optimising correctly and in good faith, and the failure lives in the space between their mandates, which is on neither scorecard.

11The aviation example, and I will keep it structural. A complex aircraft system is designed by a manufacturer and must be certified as safe by a regulator. The regulator does not have the staff or the specialist knowledge to examine every system itself, so a portion of the certification work is delegated to engineers employed by the manufacturer, who are designated to act on the regulator's behalf for that purpose.

12Consider what each party is doing. The manufacturer is producing a safe aircraft and meeting a schedule, and those are both real obligations. The regulator is ensuring safety with the resources a legislature gave it, and delegation is how it covers a workload it cannot otherwise cover. The delegated engineers are applying the standard as written. Every party is doing the job as defined.

13The seam is the question of who assesses whether the overall system, integrated, with a pilot who has been trained a particular way and holds a particular mental model of what the aircraft does, behaves safely in a situation nobody anticipated. That is not any one component's certification. It is not the manufacturer's schedule. It is not in the regulator's sampled review. It is the space between the mandates.

14And the space between mandates has a property worth understanding. It is not merely unowned, it is invisible, because every function's reporting shows its own scope and reports success within it. You can read all of the documentation from all of the parties and find no page where anybody is failing. The failure is only visible from a position none of them occupy.

15The diagnostic question for the seam is this. Take the thing that went wrong and ask which scorecard it appears on. If the honest answer is none of them, you have found a seam, and the follow-up question is who could have seen it and what it would have cost them to say so.

16The third shape is the disclosure. The act of reporting the problem is what triggers the collapse.

17The manufacturing example. A firm discovers that a component in a product already sold has a defect that appears rarely and can cause serious harm. Engineering knows the failure rate. Finance knows what a recall costs. Legal knows that a recall is an admission, that it will be exhibit one in every subsequent claim, and that the internal document estimating the failure rate will be read aloud in court.

18Notice the position this creates. The firm's own investigation, which is the responsible thing to do, has produced the most damaging document in the case, and it exists because somebody behaved well. A firm that had never measured the failure rate would have less exposure and less knowledge, and one of those two is protective.

19So the incentive structure quietly rewards not knowing. Not lying, which is a different and clearer wrong, but declining to commission the study, scoping the investigation narrowly, or asking the question verbally so that no document exists. Every one of those is legal and each makes the eventual disclosure less costly, and each also makes the harm last longer.

20Then time compounds it. On day one the firm has a defect. On day two hundred it has a defect and two hundred days of knowing, and the second is worse than the first. The disclosure becomes more expensive every day it does not happen, which means the cheapest moment to disclose is always today and it never feels like it.

21The diagnostic question for this shape is about the direction of the curve. Ask whether disclosing gets cheaper or more expensive with time. If it gets more expensive, then any delay is borrowing at a rate nobody has calculated, and the firm is not deciding whether to disclose but only when, at a price that keeps rising.

22Those are the three. The unfilled role, where the seat is occupied by somebody who cannot act. The seam, where two correct optimisations leave a gap on nobody's scorecard. And the disclosure, where telling the truth is the event.

23Ask which shape a case fits, every week. Sometimes the answer is two of them, and by week thirteen you will see all three in the same institution over a single weekend, which is why that case is last.

24One caution about how to use this. A framework that fits everything explains nothing, so if you find yourself able to assign all three shapes to every case, you are pattern-matching rather than analysing. The test of whether you have identified the shape correctly is whether it tells you what would have prevented the failure. An unfilled role is fixed by giving somebody authority. A seam is fixed by assigning the space to a named owner. A disclosure failure is fixed only by disclosing earlier, which means it is fixed long before anybody knows it is needed.

25There is a fourth thing worth saying, which is what these three shapes have in common. In every one of them, the information needed to prevent the failure existed inside the organisation before the failure happened. The risk officer knew the mark was at the wrong end of the range. Somebody knew the training assumed a mental model the system violated. Engineering knew the failure rate. None of these are failures of knowledge.

26They are failures of a specific and narrower thing: the conversion of knowledge into action by somebody with the authority to act and a reason to use it. That is why so much of this course is about incentives and reporting lines rather than about analysis. The analysis was usually done. It was done by somebody who could not act on it, and read by somebody who could and had reasons not to.

27Which suggests the question to bring to every case, underneath the three shapes. Ask who knew, ask when, and ask what it would have cost them to act on it. If the answer to the third is that it would have cost them their standing, their bonus, or their job, then you have explained the outcome, and you have also identified the thing that would have had to be different.

28That is the map. Twelve roles, the gates a release passes through, and three shapes of failure. Next week we begin with an actual company and an actual decision, and four of you will have to account for what it did. The reference sheet with the twelve functions is the reading to have open while you do.
