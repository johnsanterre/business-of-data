Reference note

Property, plant and equipment

What the accounts record

A company that buys equipment does not record the purchase as an expense in the year it pays. It records an asset, and then reduces that asset over the period the equipment is expected to be useful, charging the reduction against earnings each year. The charge is depreciation. The remaining balance is carrying value.

Cash leaves the business once, at purchase. Earnings are reduced gradually, across a period that management estimates. Those two facts explain most of what follows.

Useful life is an estimate

Useful life is how long the company expects the asset to contribute to operations, which is not the same as how long it will physically function. A processor that still runs but that no customer will rent has reached the end of its useful life while remaining perfectly operational.

The estimate is made by management, reviewed by the auditor for reasonableness, and disclosed in the accounting policies note. Accounting standards do not prescribe a number. They require that the estimate be supportable and applied consistently, and that it be revised when circumstances change.

A revision is applied going forward rather than by restating prior years. A company that shortens the life of assets it already owns spreads the remaining carrying value across the shorter remaining period, which raises the annual charge immediately.

Methods

Straight-line divides the cost, less any expected residual value, into equal annual charges. It is the most common method and the easiest to compare across companies.

Accelerated methods charge more in the early years and less later. They match the profile of an asset that loses most of its value early, which is arguably the case for equipment superseded by a new generation on a short cycle.

The choice of method and the choice of life are separate decisions, and both are disclosed.

What else the estimate affects

The estimate does not affect only the income statement. Three other things move with it.

Carrying value. A longer life leaves more value on the balance sheet at any given date, which raises total assets and book equity.

Covenants. Lending agreements test ratios computed from the borrower's accounts. Coverage and leverage tests use figures that depreciation directly affects, so a change in the estimate can move a company toward or away from a breach without anything happening in the business.

Collateral. Where equipment secures a loan, the recorded value is a reference point in the advance rate and in any recovery analysis, though a lender is free to form its own view.

Impairment is a separate test

Depreciation spreads a cost over time. Impairment asks a different question: whether the asset can still generate enough to recover what is recorded. If it cannot, the carrying value is written down at once.

The two interact. A company using a long life carries more value for longer, which leaves more exposed to an impairment charge if demand or pricing falls. The gap between recorded value and what the market would pay can persist for years without being recognised, because nothing forces recognition until either the impairment test fails or the asset is sold.

Where to find it in a filing

  • Summary of significant accounting policies. The stated method and useful life by asset category.
  • Property and equipment note. Gross cost, accumulated depreciation, and net carrying value, usually by category.
  • Management's discussion and analysis. The effect of any change in estimate, which a company that has made one is required to quantify.
  • Risk factors. Language about technological obsolescence, which is where a company acknowledges the risk it has not put into the estimate.

Reading a change in estimate

When a company extends a useful life, reported earnings rise in the year of the change and the company must disclose by how much. When a company shortens one, earnings fall. Neither movement reflects any change in the equipment or in the cash the business generates.

The useful question is not whether the estimate is aggressive but what evidence supports it, whether the company's peers agree, and what the company does when the evidence changes.
