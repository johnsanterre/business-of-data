Role briefs

CoreWeave

Issued only to the four students assigned a role.

Each brief states what your party knew in February 2025, what it did not know, the questions you should expect, and the constraint you cannot argue your way out of. You are accounting for a position that a real person held. Prepare it well enough to concede a point that is well made against you, which scores.

Role A. The chief financial officer

What you know

You know the fleet, its cost, its utilisation, and which contracts are attached to which equipment. You know that six years is within the range your peers use and that your auditor has accepted it. You know the loss widens and the covenants tighten if you shorten the life, and that you are about to ask the public to buy shares.

What you do not know

You do not know what a three-year-old processor will rent for in 2028. Nobody does. You are estimating a residual value in a market that has existed for about three years.

Expect to be asked

  1. Amazon shortened its server life six weeks ago. What do they know that you do not, and why is your answer different?
  2. Your supplier ships a new architecture every eighteen to twenty-four months. Name the evidence that a processor still earns in year six.
  3. If the estimate is correct, why does the adjusted loss remove stock compensation and leave the depreciation in?

The constraint

You cannot argue that the estimate does not matter. It sets the collateral value, and you signed the covenants.

Role B. The lender

What you know

You advanced against equipment and against contracted receivables. You wrote covenants that test coverage ratios computed from the borrower's own accounts. You have your own view of the collateral, which you formed independently and which you are not obliged to publish.

What you do not know

You do not know the resale market for a used data centre processor, because there is barely one. Your recovery analysis in a default rests on an assumption at least as soft as the borrower's.

Expect to be asked

  1. Did you underwrite to the six-year schedule, or to your own number? If your own, what is it?
  2. Seventy-seven per cent of the revenue comes from two customers. How is that priced into the facility?
  3. What happens to your position if the borrower shortens the life next year?

The constraint

You want the borrower to survive. A shorter life is more prudent and may breach the covenants you wrote, which helps nobody in the room including you.

Role C. The anchor customer

What you know

You are a large fraction of this company's revenue and you know it. You are also building your own capacity. You have your own useful life assumption for your own equipment, disclosed in your own filings, and it may not match your supplier's.

What you do not know

You do not know your own demand three years out with any precision, which is part of why you are renting rather than only building.

Expect to be asked

  1. You depreciate your own servers over six years. Does that mean you agree with this schedule, or is your fleet different?
  2. If you insource, what happens to the company whose collateral you are?
  3. Your contracts are multi-year. What are you paying for in year five?

The constraint

You benefit from your supplier being financeable. You also negotiate against it every renewal, and being its principal source of revenue is leverage you will use.

Role D. The short seller

What you know

You have read the filing carefully and you are looking for the assumption carrying the most weight with the least support. You have identified the useful life, the customer concentration, and the debt amortisation schedule, and you can see that all three depend on one another.

What you do not know

You do not know the contract terms, the utilisation, or the pricing, because they are not disclosed at that granularity. Your case is built from public documents and inference, and the inference is where you are attackable.

Expect to be asked

  1. Every hyperscaler uses five to six years. Why is this company wrong when they are not?
  2. The backlog rose from $9.9 billion to $15.1 billion. How does that fit your thesis?
  3. What would change your mind, and by when?

The constraint

You are short, so your incentive is visible to everyone in the room and will be used against you. You have to be right on the mechanism rather than merely bearish.
