Case

CoreWeave

February 2025. The registration statement is being finalised.

Every figure in this case comes from CoreWeave's public filings or from contemporaneous reporting, and each is sourced in the exhibits. The decision scene is a teaching construction: the company had already used a six-year schedule in earlier disclosures, and the case asks you to treat the assumption as though it were still open, because that is the position the finance function occupies every year when the estimate is reviewed.

The company

CoreWeave began as Atlantic Crypto, a cryptocurrency mining operation founded in 2017 by three commodities traders. The business it was in ended with the Ethereum merge in 2022, and it was left holding a large quantity of graphics processing units and the physical and operational knowledge of how to run them at scale. It converted into a cloud provider selling access to those processors, and then to newer ones, to customers training and serving machine learning models.

The conversion worked. Revenue was $16 million in 2022, $229 million in 2023, and $1.9 billion in 2024. By the end of 2024 the company held $15.1 billion in remaining performance obligations, up from $9.9 billion a year earlier.

It also lost $863 million in 2024, most of it depreciation and interest.

How the business is financed

A graphics processor is expensive, it must be bought before any revenue is earned from it, and CoreWeave was buying them in quantities that no amount of equity would cover. The company financed them with debt, using the processors themselves as collateral and the customer contracts as the source of repayment. Through 31 December 2024 it had raised total debt commitments of $12.9 billion.

Two features of that structure matter for this case. The lenders advance against the value of the equipment, so what the equipment is recorded as being worth determines how much can be borrowed and whether the covenants hold. And the loans amortise on a schedule that assumes the processors keep earning for as long as the accounts say they will.

The customers

Microsoft accounted for 62 per cent of revenue in 2024. The top two customers together accounted for 77 per cent. The second is not named in the filing.

Microsoft is also building its own capacity, and is a customer of the same supplier that sells CoreWeave its processors. The contracts are multi-year, and the backlog is real, but the concentration means that a single counterparty's decision to renegotiate or to insource would reach the income statement immediately.

The assumption in question

Every company that owns long-lived equipment must estimate how long it will remain useful, and then spread the cost across that period. The estimate is a judgment made by management, reviewed by the auditor, and disclosed. It changes nothing about the equipment. It changes reported earnings, the carrying value on the balance sheet, and therefore the collateral position and the covenant calculations.

CoreWeave depreciates technology equipment on a straight-line basis over six years. A processor bought for a given sum is written down in equal instalments across seventy-two months.

Six years is not an outlier. Microsoft moved its server life from four years to six in 2022. Alphabet moved to six in 2023. Both changes increased reported operating income substantially in the year they were made.

Two things complicate the comparison. The first is that the supplier releases a new architecture roughly every eighteen to twenty-four months, and each new generation changes what a customer will pay for access to the old one. The second is that Amazon moved in the opposite direction, shortening the life of certain servers from six years to five, effective 1 January 2025, six weeks before this case is set.

What the numbers do

The exhibits contain the depreciation sensitivity. In outline: shortening the assumed life increases annual depreciation, deepens the reported loss, reduces the carrying value of the fleet, and tightens every ratio that the lenders test. Lengthening it does the reverse. The cash position is identical in every case.

The company is about to sell shares to the public. The registration statement discloses the accounting policy, the customer concentration, and the debt. Investors will read all three.

The decision

The estimate is reviewed before the registration statement goes effective. Four parties have a position on it.

The chief financial officer must sign the filing and defend the estimate to the auditor, the board, and the market.

The lender holds the collateral and wrote covenants against the recorded values.

The anchor customer has multi-year commitments and its own view of how long the equipment it is renting will remain worth renting.

The short seller is reading the same filing and looking for the assumption that carries the most weight and the least evidence.

The case stops here.
