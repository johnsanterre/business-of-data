## Script

[P1] I am going to start with two airlines and no explanation. Northline and Crestway both operate regional routes in the same part of the country. In the same week of the same year, each of them buys the same model of used narrow-body aircraft, from the same seller, for fifty million dollars. Both put the aircraft on similar routes. Over the next three years they fly comparable schedules, at comparable load factors, and they pay comparable amounts for fuel, crew, landing fees, and maintenance. If you sat in the operations centre of either airline you would have difficulty telling them apart.

[P2] At the end of the third year, Northline reports an operating profit of four million dollars. Crestway reports an operating loss of two million. Six million dollars of difference, on two businesses that are, operationally, the same business. Before I tell you anything else, sit with that for a moment and ask yourself what could produce it. Not what did, what could. And notice that I have already ruled out most of the obvious answers. Not revenue, not fuel, not headcount, not maintenance, not the price they paid.

[P3] Here is a second pair, and again I am not going to explain it yet. Take one aircraft, Northline's, on a single afternoon in its third year of service. Northline's accounts carry that aircraft at forty-four million dollars. That morning, a broker had called with a firm offer from another operator at forty-one million. That afternoon, Northline's bank ran its own appraisal and said it would lend thirty-one million against it, and not a dollar more. Three numbers, one aircraft, one afternoon, and no dispute between the parties. Nobody in that story thinks anybody else is lying.

[P4] Now let me say out loud the thing most people conclude when they first meet this, because it is the wrong conclusion and it is worth naming precisely. The natural reading is that the accounts are attempting to say what the aircraft is worth, and are getting it somewhat wrong, and that the buyer's forty-one is the real number that the accountants have failed to reach. That reading is very sensible, it is what the word value pushes you toward, and it is not what is happening. The accounts are not trying to reach forty-one. They are answering a different question, and they are answering it correctly.

[P5] Here is the question the accounts are actually answering. Northline spent fifty million dollars of cash on one day, and that aircraft will fly for many years. If the whole fifty million were charged against the first year, that year would look catastrophic and every later year would look free, and neither impression would tell you anything useful about the business. So the accounts spread the cost across the years that get the benefit. The number on the balance sheet is not an estimate of value. It is the part of the original cost that has not yet been charged against a year. That is the whole of it, and it is the sentence to carry out of this segment.

[P6] With that in hand, go back to the first pair. Northline and Crestway differ in exactly one respect, and it is not operational. When Northline bought the aircraft, its finance team judged that the airline would fly it for twenty-five years and then sell it for about ten million. So forty million of cost gets spread across twenty-five years, which is one point six million charged every year. Crestway's finance team looked at the same airframe and judged twelve years, with the same ten million at the end. Forty million across twelve years is three point three million a year. The difference in the annual charge is one point seven million, and across three years that is five point one million, which is most of the six million gap in reported results.

[P7] Nothing else differs. The cash is identical. Both airlines paid fifty million on the same day and neither will pay another cent for the airframe. Both fly the same routes and collect the same fares. If you compared their bank statements you would see no difference at all. The entire six million sits in a judgment two groups of people made about how long they intend to fly an aeroplane, and one of them is not wrong.

[P8] I want to press on that last point, because it is the part people resist. Your instinct is that one of those two estimates must be closer to correct, and that we could find out which. But the estimate is not a prediction about metal. It is a statement about the company's own intentions and economics. If Crestway genuinely does replace its fleet every twelve years, then twelve years is the right number for Crestway, and twenty-five would be wrong for Crestway even though it is right for Northline. Two companies, identical equipment, different correct answers. That is the standard working as designed, and it is why comparing two companies' asset bases without first comparing their assumptions produces nonsense.

[P9] Now the second pair, the three numbers on one afternoon. Take them one at a time and notice what each party is actually doing. The forty-four million in the accounts is the fifty million paid, less three years at one point six. It is arithmetic on a documented cost, and Northline can produce the receipt and the schedule and show you exactly how it was built. Nobody is estimating anything at the moment the number is calculated; the only estimate was made three years earlier.

[P10] The broker's forty-one million was produced a completely different way. Somebody looked at what comparable airframes have actually sold for in recent months, adjusted for engine cycles, maintenance status, and interior condition, and named a price they think a buyer will pay. Notice what that person did not do. They did not ask what Northline paid, and they did not ask what Northline's accounts say. Those facts are irrelevant to a buyer, which means the accounting number and the market number are produced by two processes that never talk to each other. There is no mechanism forcing them to agree and no reason to expect that they will.

[P11] The bank's thirty-one million is the most interesting of the three and the least understood. The bank is not estimating today's price. It is estimating what the aircraft would fetch in a sale the bank did not choose the timing of, which is to say a sale happening because Northline stopped paying, which is to say a sale during whatever conditions caused Northline to stop paying. That is a worse day than today. So the bank takes a view of the market in a downturn and then lends less than that, and the difference between what it lends and what the asset is worth is the cushion it is buying.

[P12] Third contrast, and this one is about who can check what. Put two numbers in front of an auditor. The first is the fifty million purchase price. The auditor can verify that in an hour: there is a contract, a wire confirmation, and a bank statement. The second is the twenty-five year useful life. The auditor cannot verify that at all, because it is a statement about the future made by the people whose reported profit it determines. What the auditor can do is assess whether it is reasonable, which means whether it falls within a defensible range.

[P13] Consider what reasonable means in practice. If every comparable regional airline uses somewhere between twenty and twenty-five years, then twenty-five is reasonable more or less by definition, regardless of whether twenty-five is right. The auditor is not able to say the number is wrong; it is able to say the number is outside the range, and only then. That is a real constraint on management and it is a much weaker one than most readers assume, and it explains why the whole system is anchored on the verifiable number rather than the useful one.

[P14] Put the three contrasts together and the deep structure is visible. In every case, the difference between the numbers comes from the question each party is answering. The accounts answer how much of what we paid belongs to this year. The buyer answers what would I pay. The lender answers what could I recover on a bad day. The auditor answers can I prove this. Four questions, four numbers, one aircraft, and no dishonesty anywhere in the chain.

[P15] There is one mechanism that pulls the accounting number toward the market number, and it fires rarely by design. It is called impairment. At each reporting date the company must ask whether the asset can still generate enough over its remaining life to recover what it is carried at. If it cannot, the carrying value is written down at once, in a single charge, in that period. So the system is not blind to the market. It is patient about it, the test has a high bar, and it produces a large dated charge that is difficult to describe as anything other than an admission. Companies do not run toward it.

[P16] Now I want you to do one, and I am going to give you most of it. A shipping company buys a container vessel for eighty million dollars. It assumes a twenty-year life and expects to scrap the vessel for eight million at the end. So the amount to be spread is eighty minus eight, which is seventy-two million, and the annual charge is seventy-two divided by twenty. That is three point six million a year. After six years, accumulated depreciation is six times three point six, which is twenty-one point six million, so the carrying value is eighty minus twenty-one point six, or fifty-eight point four million.

[P17] Here is the part for you. Suppose that same company had assumed a fifteen year life instead of twenty, with the same eight million scrap value. Work out the annual charge, and then the carrying value after six years. Stop and do it before I say the answer. The annual charge is seventy-two over fifteen, which is four point eight million. Six years of that is twenty-eight point eight million, so the carrying value is fifty-one point two million rather than fifty-eight point four. Seven point two million lower, and cumulative reported profit over those six years is seven point two million lower too. Same vessel, same cash, same voyages.

[P18] The idea in this segment is one that people find genuinely uncomfortable, and I want to say that plainly rather than pretend it is obvious. It feels wrong that a company's reported profit can move by millions because of a judgment about how long it intends to keep something, with nothing physical having changed. The discomfort is appropriate and it does not go away with familiarity. What changes is that you stop being surprised by it and start checking for it, which is the actual skill.

[P19] So the habit to leave with. When somebody tells you what a company's assets are worth, ask which question they are answering. When you read a balance sheet, remember you are looking at cost minus a plan, and ask what the plan assumed. And when the number really matters, find out what a lender was willing to advance against it, because that figure was produced by somebody who has to be right rather than merely reasonable.

[P20] The next segment takes the plan apart. Twenty-five years and ten million were both chosen by a person. We will look at how that choice is made, what the alternatives to a straight line are and why almost nobody uses them, what happens to the accounts when somebody changes their mind, and why changing the estimate in one direction is far easier than changing it in the other.
