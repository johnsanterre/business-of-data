## Script

[P1] I am going to start with two airlines and no explanation. Northline and Crestway both operate regional routes in the same part of the country. In the same week of the same year, each buys the same model of used narrow-body aircraft, from the same seller, for fifty million dollars. Both put the aircraft on similar routes. Over the next three years they fly comparable schedules at comparable load factors, and they pay comparable amounts for fuel, crew, landing fees, and maintenance. If you sat in the operations centre of either airline you would have trouble telling them apart.

[P2] At the end of the third year, Northline reports an operating profit of four million dollars. Crestway reports an operating loss of two million. Six million dollars of difference, between two businesses that are, operationally, the same business. Before I tell you anything else, sit with that and ask what could produce it. Not what did, what could. And notice that I have already ruled out most of the obvious answers. Not revenue, not fuel, not headcount, not maintenance, and not the price they paid, because they paid the same price on the same day to the same seller.

[P3] Here is a second pair, and again I am not going to explain it yet. Take one aircraft, Northline's, on a single afternoon in its third year of service. Northline's accounts carry that aircraft at forty-four million dollars. That morning a broker had called with a firm offer from another operator at forty-one million. That afternoon Northline's bank ran its own appraisal and said it would lend thirty-one million against it, and not a dollar more. Three numbers, one aircraft, one afternoon. And here is the part that should bother you: there is no dispute between those parties. Nobody in that story thinks anybody else is lying or has made an error.

[P4] Now let me say out loud what most people conclude when they first meet this, because it is wrong and it is worth naming exactly. The natural reading is that the accounts are trying to say what the aircraft is worth, and are getting it somewhat wrong, and that the broker's forty-one is the real number the accountants have failed to reach. That reading is sensible. It is what the word value pushes you toward. It is also not what is happening. The accounts are not aiming at forty-one and missing. They are answering a different question, and they are answering it correctly.

[P5] Here is the question the accounts are actually answering. Northline spent fifty million dollars of cash on one day, and that aircraft will fly for many years. If the whole fifty million were charged against the first year, that year would look catastrophic and every later year would look free, and neither impression would tell you anything true about the business. So the accounts spread the cost across the years that get the benefit. The number on the balance sheet is not an estimate of value. It is the portion of the original cost that has not yet been charged against a year. That is the whole of it, and it is the sentence to carry out of this segment.

[P6] With that in hand, go back to the first pair. Northline and Crestway differ in exactly one respect and it is not operational. When Northline bought the aircraft, its finance team judged that the airline would fly it for twenty-five years and then sell it for about ten million. So forty million of cost is spread across twenty-five years, which is one point six million charged every year. Crestway's finance team looked at the same airframe and judged twelve years, with the same ten million at the end. Forty million across twelve years is three point three million a year. The difference in the annual charge is one point seven million, and over three years that is five point one million, which is most of the six million gap in reported results.

[P7] Nothing else differs. The cash is identical. Both airlines paid fifty million on the same day and neither will pay another cent for the airframe. If you compared their bank statements across those three years you would see no difference attributable to the aircraft at all. The entire six million sits in a judgment two groups of people made about how long they intend to fly an aeroplane. And here is the part that will feel wrong: neither of them is mistaken.

[P8] I want to press on that, because it is the point people resist hardest. Your instinct is that one of those estimates must be closer to correct, and that we could in principle find out which. But the estimate is not a prediction about metal. It is a statement about the company's own intentions and its own economics. If Crestway genuinely does replace its fleet every twelve years, then twelve years is right for Crestway, and twenty-five would be wrong for Crestway even though it is right for Northline. Two companies, identical equipment, two different correct answers. That is the standard working as designed, and it is why comparing two companies' asset bases without first comparing their assumptions produces nonsense.

[P9] Now to the second pair, the three numbers on one afternoon. I want to take them one at a time, from the position of the person producing each one, because each of those people is doing something different and only one of them is doing accounting.

[P10] Start at the controller's desk. The controller is responsible for what the accounts say, and the controller's constraint is that everything in them must be defensible to somebody who was not there. From that desk, the fifty million is the only number with a document behind it. There is a contract, a wire confirmation, a bank statement. Nobody has to estimate it and nobody can argue about it. The forty-four million on the books is arithmetic on that documented cost: fifty million less three years at one point six. The controller can produce the receipt and the schedule and show exactly how the number was built. At the moment of calculation nothing is being estimated at all. The only estimate was made three years earlier, once, and everything since has been division.

[P11] Move to the broker, who produced the forty-one. That number was made a completely different way. Somebody looked at what comparable airframes have actually sold for in recent months, adjusted for engine cycles, maintenance status, and interior condition, and named a price a buyer will plausibly pay. Now notice what that person did not do. They did not ask what Northline paid. They did not ask what Northline's accounts say. Both facts are irrelevant to a buyer. Which means the accounting number and the market number are produced by two processes that never communicate. There is no mechanism forcing them to agree, and no reason to expect that they will.

[P12] Third, the bank, and this is the most interesting of the three and the least understood. The bank is not estimating today's price. Follow its reasoning. It will only ever need to sell this aircraft if Northline stops paying. Northline will only stop paying under conditions bad enough to break a functioning airline. Those same conditions are very likely to be depressing the market for regional aircraft, possibly severely, because whatever is hurting Northline is probably hurting its competitors, who are the only buyers. So the bank is not asking what the aircraft is worth. It is asking what this aircraft fetches on the worst plausible day, and then it lends less than that. Thirty-one million against a forty-one million market is not pessimism. It is a priced view of a specific future.

[P13] I want to dwell on the bank for a moment, because its number is the most honest one in the story and it is the one nobody reads. The controller's number is governed by a rule. The broker's number reflects a market on a good day. The bank's number is a bet, placed with the bank's own money, on a bad one. When you genuinely want to know what a market believes an asset is worth under stress, the advance rate tells you more than any appraisal, because somebody is standing behind it and will lose if it is wrong.

[P14] Fourth position, the auditor, and here I want to give you a third contrast. Put two numbers in front of an auditor. The first is the fifty million purchase price. The auditor can verify that in an hour, because there is a contract and a wire confirmation and a bank statement. The second is the twenty-five year useful life. The auditor cannot verify that at all, because it is a statement about the future made by the people whose reported profit it determines. There is no document to check it against. There is no transaction to trace.

[P15] So what does the auditor actually do? It assesses whether the estimate is reasonable, which means whether it falls within a defensible range. Consider what that means in practice. If every comparable regional airline uses somewhere between twenty and twenty-five years, then twenty-five is reasonable more or less by definition, whether or not twenty-five is right. The auditor is not positioned to say the number is wrong. It is positioned to say the number is outside the range, and only then. That is a real constraint on management, and it is much weaker than most readers assume it is.

[P16] Fifth position, and it is the one most of you will occupy in practice: a person reading the annual report. You open the accounts and you see forty-four million. What have you been told? You have been told what the airline paid and how it has chosen to allocate that cost over time. What have you not been told? You have not been told the broker's forty-one, which appears nowhere in the primary statements. You have not been told the bank's thirty-one, which is in a credit agreement you would have to go and find. And you have not been told, in any prominent way, that twenty-five years and ten million were decisions rather than measurements.

[P17] There is one more position, and I have saved it deliberately, because everybody we have met so far is reacting to a number that somebody else chose. Now meet the person who chose it. At Northline that is the chief financial officer, and the distinction between the CFO and the controller matters here. The controller records what the policy says. The CFO sets the policy, signs the accounts, and answers for both.

[P18] So what is the CFO actually working from? More than you might expect and less than you would want. There is the fleet plan, which says when the airline intends to replace aircraft, and that is the single most relevant input because useful life is a statement about intentions. There is maintenance data on the existing fleet. There is what comparable airlines disclose, which is public and which everybody checks. And there is the manufacturer's design life, which is longer than anybody actually flies the aeroplane and is therefore of limited use. Out of those the CFO produces one number, and the honest description of that process is not calculation. It is judgment, informed by evidence, made inside a range where several answers are defensible.

[P19] Now the part that makes the seat uncomfortable. The CFO knows exactly what the number does. Twenty-five years rather than twelve moves reported profit by one point seven million a year, and the CFO can compute that in about ninety seconds. The CFO is also the person who has to present those results, meet the covenants, and explain the numbers to lenders and to a board. So the person choosing the estimate has full knowledge of its consequences and a direct interest in them. That is not a scandal and it is not avoidable, because there is nobody better placed to make the judgment. It is simply the structure, and it is why the auditor's review exists and why the disclosure requirements exist. Neither of those removes the conflict. They constrain it.

[P20] Put all five positions on the same afternoon and the structure of the thing is visible. The CFO chose twenty-five years, knowing what it does. The controller says forty-four and can prove how it was built from that choice. The broker says forty-one and is the only one making an offer. The bank says thirty-one and is the only one with money at risk in the downside. The auditor says forty-four is reasonable, which is true and is not the same as saying it is right. And the reader of the annual report sees forty-four, with no indication that the other four exist. Five questions, four numbers, one aircraft, and no dishonesty anywhere in the chain.

[P21] There is one mechanism that pulls the accounting number toward the market number, and it is worth knowing about precisely because it fires so rarely. It is called impairment. At each reporting date the company must ask whether the asset can still generate enough over its remaining life to recover what it is carried at. If it cannot, the carrying value is written down at once, in a single charge, in that period. So the system is not blind to the market. It is patient about it. The test has a high bar, it is applied when something has clearly gone wrong, and it produces a large dated charge that is hard to describe as anything other than an admission. Companies do not run toward it.

[P22] Now I want you to work one, and I am going to do most of it with you first. A shipping company buys a container vessel for eighty million dollars. It assumes a twenty-year life and expects to scrap the vessel for eight million at the end. So the amount to be spread is eighty minus eight, which is seventy-two million. The annual charge is seventy-two divided by twenty, which is three point six million a year. After six years, accumulated depreciation is six times three point six, or twenty-one point six million. So the carrying value is eighty minus twenty-one point six, which is fifty-eight point four million.

[P23] Here is the part for you. Suppose that same company had assumed a fifteen year life instead of twenty, with the same eight million scrap value. Work out the annual charge, and then the carrying value after six years. Stop the recording and do it before I give you the answer.

[P24] The annual charge is seventy-two over fifteen, which is four point eight million. Six years of that is twenty-eight point eight million. So the carrying value is eighty minus twenty-eight point eight, which is fifty-one point two million rather than fifty-eight point four. Seven point two million lower on the balance sheet, and cumulative reported profit across those six years is seven point two million lower as well. Same vessel, same cash, same voyages, same crews, same cargo. One assumption.

[P25] I want to say something about how this idea feels, because pretending it is obvious does not help anybody. It feels wrong that a company's reported profit can move by millions on a judgment about how long it intends to keep something, with nothing physical having changed and no cash having moved. That discomfort is the correct response and it does not go away with familiarity. What changes with familiarity is that you stop being surprised and start checking. That is the actual skill, and it is the reason this is the first module of the course rather than a footnote in a later one.

[P26] So the habit to leave with. When somebody tells you what a company's assets are worth, ask which of the four questions they are answering. When you read a balance sheet, remember that you are looking at cost minus a plan, and ask what the plan assumed. And when the number genuinely matters, go and find what a lender was willing to advance against it, because that figure was produced by somebody who has to be right rather than merely reasonable.

[P27] The next segment takes the plan apart. Twenty-five years and ten million were both chosen by a person. We will look at how that choice gets made, what the alternatives to a straight line are and why almost nobody uses them, what happens to the accounts when somebody changes their mind, and why changing the estimate in one direction is far easier than changing it in the other.
