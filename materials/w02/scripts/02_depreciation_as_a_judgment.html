Depreciation as a judgment

Segment 2 of 6 · How an asset is carried

The narration as written, 36 paragraphs. This is the source the audio was generated from.

1I want to start with one company and two consecutive years. Northline, the regional airline from the last segment, files its accounts for year five and reports an operating profit of six million dollars. The following year it reports seven and a half million. That is a jump of one and a half million, which for an airline of that size is a meaningful improvement, and if you were an analyst covering the stock you would want to know where it came from.

2So here is what did not change between those two years. Northline flew the same routes. It carried a comparable number of passengers at comparable fares. Fuel cost about the same. It did not reduce headcount, it did not renegotiate its leases, and it did not buy or sell a single aircraft. Its fleet on the last day of year six was exactly the fleet it had on the first day of year five. And the entire one and a half million improvement came from a decision made in a conference room in the third quarter.

3Now a second contrast, and this one is harder. Two rail freight operators, similar size, similar rolling stock, competing on overlapping routes. In the same financial year, one of them announces that it is extending the assumed useful life of its locomotives from twenty years to twenty-five. The other announces that it is shortening the life of a portion of its fleet from twenty years to sixteen. Same industry, same year, same broad type of equipment, and two companies moving in opposite directions on the identical question.

4Both of those announcements were reviewed by auditors. Both were disclosed properly. Neither company was accused of anything. Before I go on, ask yourself what could make both of those correct at the same time, because they cannot both be describing the same underlying reality about locomotives, and yet neither of them is lying.

5Here is the assumption most people bring to this, and it is worth stating plainly because it is wrong in a specific and useful way. When you hear that a company has changed an estimate, the natural conclusion is that the old estimate must have been wrong, and that if the old estimate was wrong then the numbers reported under it were also wrong, and therefore the company ought to go back and correct them. That is a completely sensible chain of reasoning. It is also not how any of this works, and the gap between what you would expect and what actually happens is where a great deal of the interesting behaviour lives.

6The rule is that a change in an accounting estimate is applied to the future and never to the past. You do not restate prior years. You do not correct anything. You take whatever carrying value remains on the books today, and you spread it across the revised remaining life, starting from the period of the change. The earlier years stay exactly as they were reported, and the reason is that they were not wrong. They were the best estimate available at the time, which is all an estimate is ever asked to be.

7Let me work Northline's revision through, slowly, because the arithmetic is the point. Recall the setup from the last segment. The aircraft cost fifty million. Northline assumed twenty-five years of life and ten million of residual value, so forty million is spread over twenty-five years at one point six million a year. By the end of year five it has charged five years at one point six, which is eight million of accumulated depreciation. The carrying value is fifty minus eight, which is forty-two million.

8Now, in year six, Northline's engineering team reports that the fleet is holding up better than expected, and the finance team revises the useful life from twenty-five years to thirty. Watch what that does. The carrying value is forty-two million. The residual value is still ten million. So the amount still to be depreciated is forty-two minus ten, which is thirty-two million. Under the old estimate that thirty-two million would have been spread over the twenty remaining years, at one point six a year. Under the new estimate it is spread over twenty-five remaining years instead.

9Thirty-two million divided by twenty-five is one point two eight million a year. The old charge was one point six. So the annual depreciation charge falls by three hundred and twenty thousand dollars, and it stays lower for the rest of the aircraft's life. Northline has several aircraft, and applying the same revision across the fleet produces the one and a half million improvement in operating profit that we started with. No cash moved. No aeroplane changed. A group of people in a room revised a number, and reported profit went up permanently.

10I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this sound sinister and it is not. If the fleet genuinely is holding up better than expected, then thirty years is a better estimate than twenty-five, and revising it is exactly what management is supposed to do. Leaving a stale estimate in place because updating it would look self-serving is its own kind of failure. The point is not that the revision is improper. The point is that a legitimate revision and a self-serving one produce identical accounting entries, and the accounts alone cannot distinguish them.

11Now run the same mechanism backwards, because the asymmetry is the thing to remember. Suppose instead that in year six Northline shortens the life from twenty-five years to eighteen. The carrying value is still forty-two million, and the amount still to depreciate is still thirty-two million. But now that thirty-two million is spread over thirteen remaining years rather than twenty. Thirty-two divided by thirteen is two point four six million a year, against the old one point six. The annual charge rises by about eight hundred and sixty thousand dollars, immediately, and stays higher.

12So extending the estimate raised profit by three hundred and twenty thousand per aircraft. Shortening it cut profit by eight hundred and sixty thousand per aircraft. Notice that the shortening hurts considerably more than the extension helped, and that is not a coincidence. Compressing the same remaining cost into fewer years is arithmetically more violent than spreading it across more.

13There is a second cost to shortening that people miss. Suppose the revision means some of the fleet will now be retired before it reaches even the shortened life, because the airline has decided to replace those aircraft sooner. Those specific assets have to be written down toward what they will be worth on the earlier retirement date, and that write-down lands as a charge in the period of the decision. So a company shortening a useful life often takes two hits at once: a permanently higher annual charge, and a one-off catch-up in the year it announces the change. Companies disclose that catch-up separately, and it is one of the most informative numbers a capital-intensive company ever publishes.

14Put the two directions side by side and you have the central asymmetry of this segment. Extending is pleasant. It raises reported earnings, it requires no cash, it can be explained in a sentence about improved maintenance outcomes, and it moves the company away from every covenant threshold at once. Shortening is expensive, immediate, visible, often accompanied by a one-off charge, and it requires the company to explain why its previous position no longer holds. Both are equally available to management. Only one of them is comfortable.

15So who actually decides, and on what basis? Let me take the same approach as the last segment and go around the people who each hold a piece of this, because they use the same phrase to mean different things.

16Start with the fleet planner, who is the person whose view matters most and who is rarely in the accounting conversation. The fleet planner owns the replacement cycle. They know that Northline's practice is to hold aircraft until the heavy maintenance check at a certain age becomes uneconomic, and that in practice this happens somewhere between year twenty-two and year twenty-eight depending on the airframe and how it has been flown. That range is the useful life, more or less directly, because useful life is defined as the period the asset is expected to be available for use by this company. The fleet plan is the primary evidence, and a useful life estimate that does not match the fleet plan is a red flag that almost nobody checks for.

17Notice what that definition does. It makes useful life a statement about the company rather than about the equipment. If Crestway from the last segment genuinely does replace its aircraft every twelve years, then twelve is right for Crestway, and it is right even though the airframe would fly for thirty. This is why the two rail operators can move in opposite directions and both be correct. One of them may have decided to hold locomotives longer as a matter of strategy, and the other may have decided to refresh its fleet faster. Each accounting change is a faithful report of a change in intention.

18Now the maintenance engineer, who holds a completely different number and is often surprised to learn that it is not the one in the accounts. The engineer thinks about design life and about airworthiness limits, which are measured in flight cycles and pressurisation cycles rather than in years. An airframe might be certified for a very large number of cycles, corresponding to thirty or thirty-five years of typical regional service. That number is real, it is documented by the manufacturer, and it is almost useless for accounting purposes, because no airline flies an aircraft until it becomes unairworthy. They sell it long before, when the maintenance economics turn.

19So when somebody says the useful life of this aircraft, at least three different professionals will give you three different numbers, all of them correct within their own frame. The engineer says thirty-five, meaning until it cannot fly. The fleet planner says twenty-five, meaning until we replace it. The accountant says twenty-five as well, but only because the accountant asked the fleet planner. If the accountant had asked the engineer, or worse, had reached for the manufacturer's design life because it was the easiest number to find, the accounts would be wrong in a way that no arithmetic check would catch.

20Let me turn now to the second decision, which is the method, and here I want to work an example in a different industry so the mechanics stand out. A trucking company buys a fleet vehicle for one hundred thousand dollars. Under straight line with a ten year life and no residual value, the charge is ten thousand dollars every year, the same in year one as in year ten, and the carrying value falls in a perfectly even staircase.

21Now do the same vehicle under an accelerated method. The common one is called declining balance. Instead of taking a fixed slice of the original cost each year, you take a fixed percentage of whatever is left. At twenty per cent declining balance, year one charges twenty per cent of one hundred thousand, which is twenty thousand, leaving eighty. Year two charges twenty per cent of eighty thousand, which is sixteen thousand, leaving sixty-four. Year three charges twelve thousand eight hundred, leaving fifty-one thousand two hundred. The charge falls every year and the carrying value drops steeply at first and then flattens out.

22Compare the two after three years. Straight line has charged thirty thousand and carries the truck at seventy thousand. Declining balance has charged forty-eight thousand eight hundred and carries it at fifty-one thousand two hundred. Now ask which of those is closer to what a three-year-old truck actually fetches, and the answer is obvious to anyone who has ever bought a used vehicle. Equipment loses a large fraction of its value in the first year or two and much less later. The accelerated curve looks like reality. The straight line does not.

23So why does almost everybody use straight line? Three reasons, and only two of them are respectable. It is simpler to administer across thousands of assets. It is comparable across companies, because there is one number to argue about rather than a curve. And, quietly, it flatters the early years, which are precisely the years in which a company that is buying assets rapidly is most likely to be raising capital. None of those reasons is illegitimate. But if you are reading the accounts of a company whose asset base is mostly new, remember that straight line is at its most generous exactly there, and that the generosity is structural rather than deliberate.

24The third estimate is residual value, and it gets the least attention of the three by a wide margin. Residual value is what the company expects to recover at the end, and it is subtracted from cost before the annual charge is worked out. Northline assumed ten million on a fifty million aircraft, so it depreciates forty. Had it assumed zero, it would depreciate the full fifty, and the annual charge would rise from one point six million to two million.

25Here is the thing to watch for. Many companies disclose a useful life and say nothing at all about residual value. The standard reading is that they are depreciating to zero, and usually that is the safe interpretation and usually it is right. But it is an inference rather than a statement. And a long useful life combined with a substantial unstated residual value is a much more aggressive position than a long life on its own, because the two assumptions compound. Thirty years to a fifteen million residual and twenty-five years to a ten million residual produce very different annual charges on the same aircraft, and only one of those numbers is usually prominent.

26There is a complication I have been suppressing, and it is time to introduce it because it changes how you read the disclosure. I have been talking as though an aircraft is one asset with one useful life. In practice, a large and complex asset is often broken into components that are depreciated separately, because the parts wear out on genuinely different schedules. The airframe might run twenty-five years. The engines are a separate asset with their own life, measured in cycles, and they are frequently overhauled or swapped rather than retired with the aircraft. The interior is refitted every seven or eight years. The avionics are replaced on their own cycle.

27This practice is called componentisation, and where it is used the accounts hold several assets where a casual reader sees one. That matters for two reasons. First, a single headline useful life in the policy note may be describing only the largest component, and the disclosure may or may not make that clear. Second, and more usefully, a company that componentises has told you something about how carefully it thinks about this, because splitting an asset into parts with separate lives is more work and produces a less flattering answer than treating the whole thing as long-lived.

28The same idea appears in every capital-intensive industry under different names. A building is split into the structure, which lasts decades, and the fit-out, roof, and mechanical systems, which do not. A ship is split into the hull and the propulsion. A data centre is split into the shell, the electrical and cooling plant, and the equipment inside it, and those three have lives that differ by a factor of ten. When you read a policy note that gives one number for a category that obviously contains parts with different lifespans, that is worth a question rather than a shrug.

29Now, who checks any of this? The auditor assesses whether the estimate is reasonable, which as we said last time means whether it falls within a defensible range. But it is worth understanding how the auditor forms a view of the range, because the mechanism has a property worth knowing. The auditor looks at what comparable companies disclose, at the company's own history, and at the evidence management has assembled. All three of those are backward-looking, and the first one in particular means that the range is set by what everybody else has already chosen.

30Consider what that implies. If an entire industry drifts toward longer lives over a decade, each individual company can point to its peers and be entirely reasonable at every step, and the range moves without any single company ever having made an unreasonable choice. There is no mechanism in the system that catches a collective drift, because reasonableness is defined relative to the collection. That is not a criticism of auditors, who are doing precisely the job they are assigned. It is a structural observation about what that job can and cannot detect.

31There is one more body that sees this, and it is the audit committee of the board. In a well run company the audit committee will be shown the significant estimates, will be told what the effect of a change is, and will ask why. In practice the quality of that conversation varies enormously, and it depends almost entirely on whether anybody on the committee understands the mechanism well enough to ask the second question. The first question, what did you change, is easy. The second question, what would have to be true for the old estimate to have been right, is the one that does the work, and it is asked much less often.

32Now one for you to do. A shipping company owns a vessel it bought for ninety million dollars. It assumed a twenty-five year life and a residual value of ten million, and it has now held the vessel for eight years. At the start of year nine, the company revises the useful life down from twenty-five years to eighteen. Work out three things: the annual charge before the revision, the carrying value at the start of year nine, and the annual charge after the revision. Stop the recording and do it.

33Here is the answer. Ninety million less ten million residual is eighty million to be spread over twenty-five years, which is three point two million a year. Eight years of that is twenty-five point six million of accumulated depreciation, so the carrying value at the start of year nine is ninety minus twenty-five point six, which is sixty-four point four million. Now subtract the ten million residual, leaving fifty-four point four million still to depreciate, and spread it over the ten remaining years of the revised eighteen year life. Fifty-four point four divided by ten is five point four four million a year. The charge has risen from three point two to five point four four, which is an increase of about seventy per cent, from a decision.

34I want to name the uncomfortable part of this segment directly, the way I did in the last one. It is that a legitimate revision and an opportunistic one leave identical traces. There is no entry in the accounts that says why. The disclosure will tell you what changed and by how much, and it will offer a sentence of rationale that the company wrote itself. What it will not tell you is whether the fleet plan actually changed first or whether the number changed first and the rationale followed. That question cannot be answered from the filing, and pretending otherwise is the most common error people make when they first learn to read this.

35So what you can do is bounded and it is still worth doing. Check the direction, because extending and shortening carry very different signals. Find the quantified effect, because companies must disclose it and it converts a policy sentence into a number you can compare against the year's reported result. Ask whether the stated rationale is about the equipment or about the company's intentions, because only the second one is what the standard is actually asking about. And look at whether the company's peers moved at the same time, because a company moving alone is telling you something and a company moving with the herd is telling you something quite different.

36The next segment changes the question. So far we have asked how the cost of an asset gets spread across time. Next we ask whether a movement in the market value of something a company owns appears in the accounts at all, and you will find that the answer depends almost entirely on a label the company applied on the day it acquired the thing.
