## Script

[P1] Useful life is the single most consequential estimate in the accounts of any company that owns a lot of equipment, and almost nobody outside the finance function knows it is an estimate at all. It is not a specification. It does not come from the manufacturer. There is no table in the accounting standards that says an aircraft is twenty years and a truck is seven. A person decides, and the person deciding works for the company whose reported profit that decision determines. This segment is about how that decision is actually made, what it does to the numbers, and what a reader can and cannot tell about it from outside.

[P2] Start with the definition, because it is narrower than most people assume. Useful life is the period over which the company expects the asset to be available for use by the company. It is not how long the thing will physically function. A delivery van that runs perfectly at nine years old but that the company always replaces at six has a useful life of six. Conversely, a company that intends to run its equipment into the ground has a longer useful life for the same equipment. The estimate is about the company's plan and the asset's economics together, not about metal fatigue.

[P3] That has a consequence people find uncomfortable when they first meet it. Two companies can own identical equipment, bought on the same day for the same price, and correctly carry it at different values, because they intend to use it differently. That is not a loophole. It is the standard working as designed, because the standard is trying to match cost against the periods that benefit. But it does mean that comparing two companies' asset bases requires you to check their useful life assumptions first, and it means that a company which changes its intentions has changed its accounting whether or not anything physical has changed.

[P4] Now the methods. The simplest and by far the most common is straight line. Take the cost, subtract whatever you expect to recover at the end, and divide by the number of years. The airline's aircraft cost fifty million with ten million expected at the end and twenty years of life, so forty million over twenty years, which is two million every year, the same charge in year one as in year nineteen. Straight line is popular for two reasons. It is easy to explain, and it is easy to compare across companies, because there is only one number to argue about rather than a curve.

[P5] The alternative family is accelerated methods, which charge more in the early years and less later. The most common is called declining balance, where instead of taking a fixed slice of the original cost each year, you take a fixed percentage of whatever is left. Take a trucking company with a fleet vehicle that cost one hundred thousand dollars, on a declining balance at forty per cent. Year one charges forty thousand, leaving sixty. Year two charges forty per cent of sixty, which is twenty-four thousand, leaving thirty-six. Year three charges fourteen thousand four hundred. The charge falls every year, steeply at first, and the carrying value drops fast and then flattens.

[P6] The reason to care about the choice is that accelerated methods produce a carrying curve that looks much more like what actually happens to the resale value of most equipment. A truck loses a large fraction of its value in the first year and much less in the seventh. A straight line, by construction, cannot do that. It writes off the same amount in the first year as in the last, which means it consistently overstates carrying value early in an asset's life and understates it late. For assets whose market value really does fall in a straight line, this does not matter. For assets that lose value fastest when they are newest, straight line systematically carries them above what they would fetch, and the gap is widest in the years right after purchase.

[P7] So why does almost everybody use straight line anyway? Partly inertia and comparability. Partly because it is administratively simpler across thousands of assets. And partly, it must be said, because it flatters the early years, which are the years a growing company is most likely to be raising money in. None of those reasons is illegitimate. But if you are reading the accounts of a company that is buying assets rapidly, the choice of straight line is doing more work than it appears to, because a company whose fleet is mostly new is a company whose carrying values are mostly in the region where straight line is most generous.

[P8] Next, the third estimate, which gets the least attention and deserves more. Residual value is what the company expects the asset to be worth at the end of its useful life, and it is subtracted from cost before the annual charge is worked out. The airline assumed ten million on a fifty million aircraft, so it depreciates forty. If it had assumed a residual of zero, it would depreciate the full fifty, and the annual charge would rise from two million to two and a half. A residual value assumption of zero is the conservative choice and it is also, quietly, quite common, because a company that states no residual value cannot be accused of assuming one that does not materialise.

[P9] Here is the thing to look for. When a company discloses a useful life but says nothing about residual value, the standard reading is that it is depreciating to zero. That is usually the safe interpretation and usually the right one. But it is an inference rather than a statement, and it is worth confirming, because a long useful life combined with a substantial unstated residual value is a much more aggressive position than a long useful life alone. The two assumptions compound. Twenty years to a ten million residual and thirty years to a fifteen million residual produce very different annual charges on the same aircraft, and only one of those numbers is usually prominent.

[P10] Now the part that matters most in practice, which is what happens when an estimate changes. Companies revise useful lives. Circumstances change, technology changes, and the original estimate may simply have been wrong. The accounting treatment for such a revision is specific and it surprises people. It is applied prospectively. You do not restate prior years. You take whatever carrying value is left, and you spread it over the revised remaining life, starting now.

[P11] Work it through on the aircraft. Five years in, the carrying value is forty million, and the airline decides the aircraft will actually last twenty-five years rather than twenty. The remaining life goes from fifteen years to twenty. The amount still to be depreciated is forty million less the ten million residual, so thirty million, and instead of spreading that over fifteen years at two million a year, it now spreads over twenty years at one and a half million a year. Annual depreciation falls by five hundred thousand. Reported profit rises by five hundred thousand every year from now on. Nothing happened to the aircraft. Somebody changed their mind about it, and the change was recorded by making future earnings larger.

[P12] Run the same mechanism in reverse and you can see why companies are reluctant. Suppose instead the airline shortens the life from twenty years to fifteen at the five-year mark. Thirty million still to depreciate, now over ten years rather than fifteen, so three million a year instead of two. Annual profit falls by a million, immediately and for the rest of the asset's life. And if the company also concludes that some of its fleet should be retired early, it may take an additional catch-up charge in the period of the change for the equipment that will not reach even the shortened life. Shortening is expensive and visible. Extending is pleasant and easy to explain as prudent updating.

[P13] Because of that asymmetry, a change in useful life is one of the most informative disclosures a capital-intensive company makes, and the direction tells you a great deal. An extension arrives with a rationale about improved maintenance practices or observed longevity, and it raises reported earnings in the year it is made. A shortening arrives with a rationale about the pace of technological change, and it lowers them. Companies are required to disclose the effect of a change in estimate when it is material, which means the number is usually findable. Look for it, because it converts a policy sentence into a quantity.

[P14] There is one more asymmetry worth naming. Extensions tend to happen when a company has an incentive for higher reported earnings, which is when it is raising capital, defending a valuation, or approaching a covenant. Shortenings tend to happen when a company has already absorbed bad news and would rather take the hit now. Neither pattern proves anything about any particular company. Both are worth holding in mind, because a change in estimate is one of the few accounting decisions that a company makes entirely on its own timing.

[P15] The last thing to say is about who checks. The auditor reviews the estimate for reasonableness. That is a real constraint and it is a weak one. Reasonableness is a range, not a point, and if every comparable company in the industry uses six years then six years is reasonable almost by definition, regardless of whether six years is right. Where the auditor considers the estimate especially difficult or judgmental, it may be identified in the audit report as a matter requiring particular attention. That designation is a signal worth taking seriously, because it is the auditor saying, in the only language available to it, that this number was hard.

[P16] To summarise the mechanics. Useful life is management's estimate of its own intentions and the asset's economics. The method determines the shape of the charge, and straight line is generous early. Residual value reduces the amount depreciated and is often unstated. A revision is applied to the future rather than the past, which means extending a life raises earnings from the moment of the decision onward. And the check on all of it is an auditor assessing whether the estimate falls within a reasonable range, in an industry where the range is set by what everybody else has already chosen.
