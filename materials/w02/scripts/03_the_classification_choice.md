## Script

[P1] So far this module has treated one question: how the cost of an asset gets spread across time. This segment treats a different one. When the market value of something a company owns moves, does that movement appear in the accounts, and if so, where? The answer depends almost entirely on a label the company applied when it acquired the thing. Same asset, same market, same price move, and two companies can report completely different numbers because they classified it differently on day one. That is not a defect anyone is hiding. It is written into the standards, it is disclosed, and it is one of the most powerful levers in financial reporting.

[P2] I need to separate two regimes here, because they are routinely confused and the confusion causes real errors. Held-to-maturity and available-for-sale are classifications for **debt securities**: bonds, notes, and similar instruments. They do not apply to equipment. Equipment and other long-lived assets have their own, much simpler classification question, which is whether the company intends to keep using the asset or intends to sell it. Both regimes answer the same underlying question about whether market movements hit the accounts. They answer it differently, and knowing which regime you are in is the first thing to establish.

[P3] Take the securities regime first, because it is where the mechanism is starkest. A company buys a bond for one hundred million dollars. At the reporting date, interest rates have risen and the bond would now sell for eighty-eight million. Whether that twelve million appears anywhere depends on a decision the company made when it bought it. If it classified the bond as trading, the loss goes straight through the income statement and reduces reported profit. If it classified the bond as available for sale, the loss appears in equity, in a section reported below net income, and does not touch profit at all. If it classified the bond as held to maturity, the loss appears nowhere. The bond stays on the books at what is called amortised cost, near the original hundred million, and the twelve million shows up only as a disclosure in the notes.

[P4] Look at what that means. Three companies, identical bond, identical market move. One reports a twelve million loss in profit. One reports no effect on profit but a reduced equity balance. One reports essentially nothing, with the gap visible only to a reader who goes into the notes and looks for it. Every one of those treatments is correct. The difference is entirely in the intention the company declared and documented.

[P5] The held-to-maturity classification comes with a condition, and the condition is the interesting part. To use it, the company must have both the intent and the ability to hold the security until it matures. Intent is a management assertion. Ability is a question about liquidity, because a company that might be forced to sell to raise cash does not have the ability, whatever it intends. And the classification has teeth. If a company sells a meaningful amount of what it had designated held to maturity, it can be barred from using the classification for a period, which forces the rest of the portfolio into a category where the losses become visible. So the label is not merely descriptive. It is a commitment, and breaking it is costly in a way that is designed to stop companies from using the label opportunistically.

[P6] Hold that structure in your head, because it produces a specific and dangerous failure mode. A company that has classified a large portfolio as held to maturity, and whose securities have fallen substantially in value, is carrying an unrecognised loss that is disclosed but not booked. As long as it holds, that is fine and it is exactly what the classification is for. The moment it is forced to sell, the loss becomes real, immediate, and public. The classification does not reduce the loss. It defers the recognition of the loss, and it defers it in a way that couples the accounting to the company's liquidity. When liquidity goes, the accounting goes with it, at the worst possible moment. We will come back to that in week thirteen, where it is the whole story.

[P7] Now the other regime, for equipment and other long-lived assets. Here there is no held-to-maturity and no available-for-sale. There are two states. An asset is held for use, or it is held for sale. Almost everything is held for use, which is the default, and a held-for-use asset is carried at cost less accumulated depreciation, exactly as the previous segments described. Market movements do not touch it. If the aircraft the airline paid fifty million for is now worth forty-one, the accounts continue to show whatever the depreciation schedule says, and the forty-one appears nowhere.

[P8] The held-for-sale classification is narrow and it triggers a different treatment. To use it the company must be committed to a plan to sell, the asset must be available for immediate sale, and a sale must be probable within about a year. Once an asset is classified held for sale, two things change. Depreciation stops, because the asset is no longer being consumed in operations. And the asset is measured at the lower of its carrying amount or its fair value less costs to sell, which means that for the first time market value can force the number down. Reclassifying to held for sale is therefore often the moment a company recognises a loss it has been carrying for years.

[P9] For held-for-use assets, the only route by which market reality reaches the accounts is impairment, which the first segment introduced. It is worth being precise about how high that bar is, because people assume it is more sensitive than it is. The usual test proceeds in two steps. First, compare the carrying amount against the total undiscounted cash flows the asset is expected to generate over its remaining life. Undiscounted means you simply add up the future cash without reducing it for the time value of money, which is a deliberately forgiving comparison. Only if the carrying amount exceeds that sum do you proceed to the second step, which measures the actual write-down against fair value.

[P10] The consequence is that a long-lived asset can be worth considerably less than its carrying value in the market and still pass the impairment test comfortably, because the test is not asking about market value. It is asking whether the asset will pay for itself over its remaining life without discounting. An asset generating steady cash can fail badly on resale value and never trip the test. This is why the gap between carrying value and market value can persist for years in exactly the companies where it matters most.

[P11] Pull the two regimes together, because the common structure is what to remember. In both cases, a classification made at acquisition determines whether subsequent market movements are recognised. In both cases, the classification that defers recognition requires a statement of intent by management. In both cases, the deferral is legitimate while the intent holds and collapses when it does not. Securities held to maturity become a realised loss when liquidity forces a sale. Equipment held for use becomes a write-down when a sale is planned or when the cash it generates stops covering what it is carried at.

[P12] So when you read a balance sheet, two questions travel together. First, which classification is each significant asset in, and what did that classification require the company to assert about its own intentions. Second, what is the gap between carrying value and market value that the classification is holding out of the accounts, and can you find it in the notes. The gap is often disclosed. It is disclosed somewhere that does not affect any headline number, which is precisely the point of the classification, and it is available to anyone who goes looking.
