## Script

[P1] Two shipping companies, Harbourline and Meridian. Each owns four identical container vessels, bought new in the same year for eighty million dollars apiece. Each financed them the same way, with a loan covering sixty per cent of the cost. Each charters its vessels on similar terms to similar customers and earns similar revenue. Both are profitable at the operating level, both are current on every payment, and neither has missed a single interest date.

[P2] In year seven, Harbourline receives a letter from its lender stating that it is in breach of a financial covenant and that the lender reserves all of its rights, including the right to demand immediate repayment of the entire facility. Meridian receives no such letter. Nothing has happened to Meridian's vessels that has not happened to Harbourline's. Neither company has lost a customer. The freight market treated them identically.

[P3] The difference is that Harbourline depreciates its vessels over twenty-five years and Meridian over eighteen. That is the whole of it. And I want you to notice something that will feel upside down: the company using the **longer** life, which reports **higher** profits and carries its vessels at a **higher** value, is the one that got the letter. Before I explain, sit with that, because the obvious guess is the wrong way round.

[P4] Here is the assumption to dismantle. People treat an accounting estimate as something that lives inside a document. It changes what the accounts say, and what the accounts say is a description of the business, and descriptions do not do anything. That is true right up until somebody lends money against the description and writes a contract that converts it into an obligation. At that point the estimate stops describing the company and starts governing what the company is permitted to do.

[P5] Let me build up to Harbourline's letter properly, starting with why any of this is financed with debt in the first place. The basic choice is equity or debt. Equity is expensive and permanent: you sell a share of the company, you never repay it, and the people who bought it expect a higher return than any lender would demand, because they stand behind the lenders if things go wrong. Debt is cheaper and temporary: you pay interest, you repay principal on a schedule, and if you miss either the lender has rights. The cheapness of debt is not a gift. It is compensation for accepting a schedule.

[P6] For a company buying long-lived equipment the pull toward debt is strong, and the main reason is not cost. It is matching. A vessel earns cash over twenty-odd years. A loan repaid over fifteen lines those two streams up, so the vessel substantially pays for itself as it sails. Funding it with equity instead means the shareholders carry the entire cost on day one and wait two decades to be repaid through earnings. Lining up the life of the funding with the life of the asset is the most reliable idea in asset finance, and almost everything else is a refinement of it.

[P7] Now the form. These loans are almost always secured, meaning the asset itself is the collateral. If the borrower stops paying, the lender takes the vessel and sells it. That structure lets the lender charge less, because its downside is bounded by something it can seize rather than by the general fortunes of the business. It also makes the lender intensely interested in one question, which is what the collateral fetches in a sale it did not choose the timing of.

[P8] The lender expresses that interest through the **advance rate**. Sixty per cent against an eighty million vessel means forty-eight million advanced and thirty-two million of the borrower's own money underneath it. That thirty-two million is the equity cushion, and its job is to absorb the difference between what the lender thinks the vessel is worth and what it turns out to be worth when sold in a hurry. As I said two segments ago, the advance rate is the lender's honest opinion of the asset, expressed as a number, by somebody with money at stake.

[P9] Which brings us to covenants. A covenant is a promise in the loan agreement, and there are two broad families. **Affirmative** covenants say the borrower will do things: maintain insurance, deliver audited accounts within ninety days, keep the vessels classed and in good repair. **Negative** covenants say the borrower will not: take on more debt beyond a stated level, sell material assets, pay dividends above a threshold. Both are enforced the same way. Breaching one is an event of default, which typically entitles the lender to demand the whole loan back immediately.

[P10] The family that matters here is the financial covenants, because they are computed from the accounts. Three are close to universal. A **leverage** covenant compares total debt against earnings and requires the ratio to stay below a ceiling. A **coverage** covenant compares earnings or cash flow against the interest and principal due and requires the ratio to stay above a floor. And a **loan to value** covenant compares the outstanding loan against the value of the collateral and requires that ratio to stay below a ceiling.

[P11] Now connect this back to the first two segments, because here is the join. A leverage covenant computed on earnings **before** depreciation is deliberately insulated from the depreciation estimate, and lenders write it that way for exactly that reason. A coverage covenant computed from operating income is **not** insulated, because operating income is after depreciation. And a loan to value covenant is not insulated at all, because it runs directly off the value of the collateral.

[P12] So which value? This is the detail that decides Harbourline's fate and it is the thing most people get wrong. Some loan to value covenants are computed against the **carried** value in the borrower's accounts. Others are computed against an **appraised** value obtained from an independent valuer, often annually, and often at the borrower's expense. Those two produce very different outcomes, and which one you have is a matter of what was negotiated, and it is in the definitions section of the credit agreement rather than in the body.

[P13] Harbourline's covenant is appraisal-based, and now the story resolves. Work the numbers. Harbourline's vessels cost eighty million each. It borrowed forty-eight million against each, on a fifteen year amortising loan, so it repays about three point two million of principal a year. After seven years it has repaid about twenty-two million, leaving roughly twenty-six million outstanding per vessel.

[P14] Now the value. Container vessel prices are cyclical, and in year seven the market is soft. An independent appraiser values each vessel at thirty-four million. So the loan to value ratio is twenty-six divided by thirty-four, which is about seventy-six per cent. Harbourline's covenant caps loan to value at seventy-five. It is in breach by one point, and every number in that calculation came from the market and from the amortisation schedule.

[P15] So where does depreciation come in? Not into the ratio. Into everything around it. Harbourline, on a twenty-five year schedule, carries each vessel at about fifty-eight million in its own accounts. The appraiser says thirty-four. That is a twenty-four million gap per vessel, ninety-six million across the fleet, and it has been widening for years in plain sight of anybody who compared the two numbers. Meridian, on an eighteen year schedule, carries each vessel at about fifty-one million, which is still above the appraisal but by considerably less.

[P16] And here is the consequence that matters. Because Harbourline's own accounts said the vessels were worth fifty-eight million, nobody inside Harbourline was worried. The internal reporting showed comfortable asset cover. The covenant test uses a different number, computed once a year by an outsider, and it is the only number in the whole arrangement that was tracking reality. Meridian's shorter schedule did not protect Meridian from the market. It meant Meridian's own accounts were closer to the truth, so Meridian saw it coming and raised equity in year six.

[P17] Let me take the vantage points, because three people look at this from very different places. Start with the borrower's treasurer, who has an incentive that is entirely legible. Extending a useful life raises operating income, which helps the coverage covenant. It slows the decline in carrying value, which makes the internal picture look better. It requires no cash, and it can be justified in a sentence. There is no version of this where the treasurer is unaware of that. The question a board should ask is not whether the treasurer knew, but what evidence was assembled before the decision rather than after.

[P18] Second, the credit officer who drafts the covenant. This person's whole professional life is spent anticipating the previous paragraph. They know the borrower chooses the depreciation estimate. So they write the leverage test on earnings before depreciation, precisely to take that lever away, and they insist on independent appraisal for the loan to value test, precisely because they will not accept a valuation the borrower controls. Every one of those choices is a defensive response to the fact that the borrower writes its own accounts. When you read a covenant package, you are reading a lender's list of the things it does not trust.

[P19] Third, and the one nobody thinks about, the workout banker. This is the person who gets the file after the breach. Their view is unsentimental. Seizing four vessels is slow, expensive, and produces a worse recovery than a performing loan, because a forced sale into a soft market is precisely how you get the lowest possible price. So their strong preference is not to seize. Their preference is to use the breach as leverage to improve their position: a higher margin, a fee, tighter reporting, additional security, or an equity injection from the shareholders.

[P20] That is why most covenant breaches do not end in seizure. They end in a negotiation and a waiver or an amendment, and the cost to the borrower is real but is measured in basis points and in lost freedom rather than in liquidation. The exception, and it is an important one, is when the lender has already decided it wants out of the exposure. In that case the covenant is not a warning system. It is the mechanism that lets the lender leave, and the borrower discovers that its lender's intentions changed some time ago.

[P21] There is a fourth party, and it sits above all three: the rating agency. Where a company issues public debt, an agency assigns a rating that determines what the company pays to borrow, and the agency does something the accounts do not. It publishes its own adjustments. It will take the reported figures, add back operating leases, apply its own view of asset lives where it disagrees with the company's, and compute leverage on its adjusted numbers rather than the reported ones. Those adjustments are published in the agency's methodology and in its reports on the specific company, which makes them one of the few places where somebody with no incentive to flatter has written down what they think the real numbers are. If you can find a rating report on a company you are analysing, read the adjustments before you read the opinion.

[P22] There is also a mechanic underneath the loan to value test that is worth understanding, because it operates continuously rather than annually. It is called the borrowing base. In a facility of this kind the amount the borrower may have drawn at any moment is capped by a formula applied to the eligible collateral, typically a percentage of appraised value, sometimes stepping down as assets age. If the collateral value falls, the borrowing base falls with it, and if the borrower is drawn above the new base it must repay the difference immediately. That is not a covenant breach with a negotiation attached. It is a cash call, and it arrives on the schedule the formula dictates rather than when anybody has decided anything.

[P23] So there are really two ways an asset value can reach out and touch a company. The slow way is a covenant tested annually or quarterly, where a breach starts a conversation. The fast way is a borrowing base that recalculates and demands cash. Companies that fail in this pattern are frequently undone by the second rather than the first, because the second gives them no time.

[P24] There are two structures I need to add, because they change who bears what and both are common. The first is ring-fencing. Instead of borrowing against an asset it owns directly, a company can put the asset into a separate legal entity created for the purpose, and have that entity borrow. The lender's claim is against the entity and the asset inside it, and typically not against the parent. This lets the parent isolate the risk, and it also means the parent's headline balance sheet may not show the whole picture, because whether the obligation appears depends on consolidation rules rather than on obviousness.

[P25] The second is commitments that behave like debt without being called debt. A long-term lease on a facility. A take-or-pay contract for power, where you pay whether you use it or not. A purchase commitment for equipment to be delivered over several years. Each of those obliges the company to pay cash on a schedule it cannot easily escape, which is the economic definition of debt. The accounting for each has changed over time and varies by type, so the reliable move is not to reason about the accounting. It is to read the contractual obligations table in the annual report, which sets out what must be paid and when, regardless of where the obligation sits.

[P26] Now let me work a covenant calculation with you, because seeing the arithmetic makes the connection concrete. A haulage company has debt of one hundred and twenty million. Its operating income before depreciation is forty million, and its depreciation charge is twenty-two million, so its operating income after depreciation is eighteen million. Its interest cost is twelve million.

[P27] Its leverage covenant caps debt at three times earnings before depreciation. One hundred and twenty divided by forty is three point zero, so it is exactly at the limit and not in breach. Its coverage covenant requires operating income after depreciation to be at least one point four times interest. Eighteen divided by twelve is one point five, so it has headroom, but not a great deal.

[P28] Now the company shortens the useful life of its fleet, and the depreciation charge rises from twenty-two million to twenty-eight million. Watch which test moves. Earnings before depreciation is unchanged at forty, so leverage is still three point zero and the leverage covenant is untouched, exactly as the lender designed. But operating income after depreciation falls from eighteen million to twelve million. Twelve divided by twelve is one point zero, against a floor of one point four. The company is in breach of its coverage covenant, and nothing has happened to the trucks, the customers, the revenue, or the cash.

[P29] One more piece of the same picture, because coverage is not the only test that moved. Suppose that haulage company also has an asset cover test, requiring its debt of one hundred and twenty million to be no more than seventy per cent of the appraised value of its fleet. Its fleet is appraised at one hundred and eighty million. One hundred and twenty divided by one hundred and eighty is sixty-seven per cent, so it sits inside the seventy per cent limit with a little room.

[P30] Now let the freight market soften and the next appraisal come in at one hundred and sixty-five million. One hundred and twenty divided by one hundred and sixty-five is seventy-three per cent. The company is in breach, and notice what produced it. Not a decision, not a transaction, not anything the company did. An outside valuer wrote down a different number, and the company's own accounts, still carrying the fleet on a long schedule well above the appraisal, gave it no warning at all that this was coming.

[P31] Here is one for you. The same haulage company, but now consider the reverse. Suppose instead it **extends** the fleet life so that depreciation falls from twenty-two million to sixteen million. Work out the new operating income after depreciation, the new coverage ratio, and whether the leverage covenant moves. Stop the recording and do it.

[P32] The answer. Operating income after depreciation rises from eighteen million to twenty-four million, because six million less is being charged. The coverage ratio is twenty-four divided by twelve, which is two point zero, against a floor of one point four. The company has gone from one point five of headroom to two point zero, a substantial improvement, with no change in cash whatsoever. And the leverage covenant is exactly where it was, at three point zero, because earnings before depreciation never moved. One decision, one covenant improved, one covenant untouched, and no cash.

[P33] I want to name the uncomfortable thing in this segment, as I have in the others. It is that the borrower controls an input to a test the borrower must pass. Lenders know this, and their entire covenant architecture is built around it: earnings before depreciation for leverage, independent appraisal for asset cover, audited accounts delivered on a deadline. Those defences work reasonably well. What they cannot do is stop a company from carrying assets far above what they would fetch, and reassuring itself with its own numbers, right up until an outside valuer produces a different one.

[P34] So the things worth checking, and they are all available. Find the covenants, which means the definitions section of the credit agreement rather than the annual report, because agreements are filed as exhibits and are almost never read. Work out which of them depreciation touches and which it does not. Establish whether asset cover is tested against carried value or appraised value, because that single word decides whether the company is marking its own homework. And compare the carrying value of the asset base against any independent valuation you can find, because that gap is the thing the covenant will eventually notice.
