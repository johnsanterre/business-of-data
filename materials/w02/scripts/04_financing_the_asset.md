## Script

[P1] Everything so far has been about how a number gets onto the balance sheet. This segment is about why that number has teeth. On its own, a carrying value is an accounting artefact and nothing more. It becomes consequential the moment somebody lends against it, because at that point the number stops describing the company's own view of its assets and starts governing what the company is contractually permitted to do. That is the mechanism that turns an estimate into an event.

[P2] Start with the basic choice, which is whether to fund an asset with equity or with debt. Equity is expensive and permanent. You sell a share of the company, you never have to give it back, and the people who bought it expect a return higher than any lender would demand, because they are behind the lenders in the queue if things go wrong. Debt is cheaper and it is temporary. You pay interest, you repay principal on a schedule, and if you miss either one the lender has rights. The cheapness of debt is not a free lunch. It is compensation for accepting a schedule.

[P3] For a company buying long-lived equipment, there is a strong pull toward debt, and it is not primarily about cost. It is about matching. The asset generates cash over many years. A loan repaid over many years lines those two streams up. Funding a twenty-year aircraft with equity means the shareholders carry the whole cost up front and wait two decades to be repaid through earnings. Funding it with a fifteen-year loan means the aircraft substantially pays for itself as it flies. The principle is called matching the tenor of the funding to the life of the asset, and it is the single most reliable idea in asset finance.

[P4] Now, the specific form this usually takes is a secured loan, where the asset itself is the collateral. The lender's position is that if the borrower stops paying, the lender takes the asset and sells it. That structure lets the lender charge less, because its downside is bounded by something it can seize rather than by the general fortunes of the business. It also makes the lender extremely interested in one question, which is what the collateral would fetch in a sale it did not choose the timing of.

[P5] The lender expresses that interest through the advance rate. If a lender will lend seventy per cent against an aircraft appraised at fifty million, it advances thirty-five million and the borrower puts in fifteen. That fifteen million is the equity cushion, and its purpose is to absorb the difference between what the lender thinks the asset is worth and what it turns out to be worth when sold in a hurry. The size of the cushion is the lender's real opinion about the asset, expressed as a number, and it is usually a more honest opinion than anything in the borrower's accounts, because the lender has money at risk in exactly the scenario the accounts are silent about.

[P6] Take a shipping company as a second example, because the dynamics are cleaner. A container vessel costs eighty million. A lender advances sixty per cent, so forty-eight million, on a twelve-year term with the vessel as security. Vessel values are cyclical and everybody in the industry knows it, so the lender does not lend against today's appraisal alone. It lends against a view of the trough. The advance rate is doing the work that the borrower's depreciation schedule does not do, which is to price the possibility that the asset is worth much less at exactly the moment the lender needs it to be worth something.

[P7] Which brings us to covenants. A covenant is a promise in the loan agreement about what the borrower will do, or about what the borrower's financial statements will show. There are two broad families. Affirmative covenants say the borrower will do things: maintain insurance, deliver audited accounts within ninety days, keep the collateral in good repair. Negative covenants say the borrower will not: incur additional debt beyond a stated level, sell material assets, pay dividends above a threshold. Both are enforced by the fact that breaching them is an event of default, which typically lets the lender demand immediate repayment of the whole loan.

[P8] The family that matters for this module is the financial covenants, because they are computed from the accounts. Two are nearly universal. A leverage covenant compares total debt against earnings, often earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, and requires the ratio to stay below some ceiling. A coverage covenant compares earnings or cash flow against the interest and principal due, and requires the ratio to stay above some floor. There is usually also a loan-to-value covenant, which compares the outstanding loan against the appraised or carried value of the collateral, and requires that ratio to stay below a ceiling.

[P9] Now connect that back to the previous segments, because this is the join. A leverage covenant that uses earnings before depreciation is deliberately insulated from the depreciation estimate, and lenders write it that way for exactly that reason. But a coverage covenant computed from operating income is not insulated, because operating income is after depreciation. And a loan-to-value covenant computed from the carried value of the collateral is not insulated at all. It runs directly off the number that management's useful life estimate determines.

[P10] So consider what happens if the airline shortens the useful life of its fleet. Annual depreciation rises. Operating income falls, so any covenant computed from operating income moves toward its floor. Carrying value falls faster, so the loan-to-value ratio rises toward its ceiling. Nothing has changed about the aircraft, the routes, the passengers, or the cash. A management estimate moved, and the company moved closer to a technical default on both tests. That is the reason a company with substantial secured debt has a direct financial interest in the length of its own useful life estimates, and it is why the estimate is not a purely technical matter for the accounting team.

[P11] Run it the other way and the incentive is plainer still. Extending a useful life raises operating income, slows the decline in carrying value, and moves the company away from both covenant thresholds at once. It costs nothing, requires no cash, and can be justified with a paragraph about improved maintenance practice. It is one of the few levers available to a finance function that improves several constraints simultaneously without doing anything.

[P12] A word on what a breach actually means, because people overestimate the drama. Most covenant breaches do not end in seizure. They end in a negotiation. The lender has the right to accelerate, and it usually prefers not to, because seizing collateral is slow, expensive, and produces a worse recovery than a performing loan. So the normal outcome is a waiver or an amendment, in exchange for a higher rate, a fee, tighter terms, or additional security. The breach is real and it is costly, but it is costly in basis points rather than in liquidation. The exception is when the lender has already decided it wants out, in which case the covenant is the mechanism that lets it leave.

[P13] There is one more structure worth knowing, because it is common in equipment finance and it changes who bears what. Instead of borrowing against an asset it owns, a company can put the asset into a separate legal entity created for the purpose, and have that entity borrow. The lender's claim is against the entity and the asset inside it, and typically not against the parent. That is called ring-fencing, and it lets the parent isolate the risk. It also means the parent's headline balance sheet may not show the full picture, because the obligation sits in a structure whose disclosure is a matter of consolidation rules rather than of obviousness.

[P14] Related, and easy to miss, are commitments that behave like debt without appearing as debt. A long-term lease on a facility, a take-or-pay contract for power, a purchase commitment for equipment to be delivered over several years. Each of these obliges the company to pay cash on a schedule it cannot easily escape, which is the economic definition of debt, and the accounting for each has changed over time and varies by type. The reliable move is to read the contractual obligations table in the annual report, which sets out what the company must pay and when, regardless of where those obligations sit on the balance sheet.

[P15] The commercial property lender is the cleanest illustration of everything in this segment operating at once. It lends against a building at a sixty-five per cent advance rate, with a loan-to-value covenant at seventy-five per cent and an interest coverage covenant. The building's carrying value falls slowly on a long depreciation schedule. Its market value falls fast when interest rates rise, because the rent it generates is now worth less. The covenant is computed against appraised value rather than carried value, precisely because the lender knows the carried value will not tell it anything in time. And when the appraisal comes in low, the borrower must either put in more equity or find itself in breach, without a single tenant having moved out.

[P16] The summary is short. Debt is matched to asset life because that is what makes the arithmetic work. Secured debt makes the lender an interested party in the value of a specific asset, and the advance rate is the lender's honest opinion of it. Covenants convert accounting numbers into contractual obligations, and some of those covenants run directly off the estimate that management chooses. Which means that a decision about useful life, made by the finance function and reviewed for reasonableness, propagates into the terms on which the company is permitted to keep operating.
