## Script

[P1] Everything the previous segments described is disclosed. Not summarised, not implied, disclosed, in specific places, in every annual report and every registration statement filed by a company of any size. This segment is about where those places are, in what order to visit them, and what to do when the thing you are looking for is not there. It is a navigation exercise rather than a conceptual one, and it is the difference between reading about a company and reading a company.

[P2] Start with the summary of significant accounting policies, which is almost always the first or second note to the financial statements. Find the paragraph headed property and equipment, or property, plant and equipment. It will be short, often four or five sentences, and it will tell you three things. The measurement basis, which will say cost less accumulated depreciation. The method, which will say straight line unless something unusual is going on. And the useful lives, given as a range or as a list by asset category. That paragraph is the policy. Everything else in this module is an elaboration of it.

[P3] Read the categories carefully rather than the headline range. A filer will often say something like buildings thirty to forty years, leasehold improvements the shorter of the lease term or useful life, and equipment three to ten years. A range of three to ten is not informative on its own. What you want is the category that holds the assets which actually generate the revenue, and the specific life assigned to it. If the disclosure is too coarse to tell you that, note the coarseness, because a company with one dominant asset class and a wide undifferentiated range has told you less than it appears to.

[P4] Second stop is the property and equipment note itself, usually a table further into the notes. It gives gross cost by category, accumulated depreciation, and the net carrying value that appears on the balance sheet. This table is more useful than it looks. Gross cost against accumulated depreciation tells you roughly how far through its life the asset base is. A company whose accumulated depreciation is ten per cent of gross cost has a young fleet. One where it is seventy per cent has an old one and a large replacement bill coming. The same net carrying value can sit on either situation, and the balance sheet alone will not distinguish them.

[P5] Third stop is management's discussion and analysis, and specifically anywhere it discusses a change in estimate. If the company revised a useful life during the period, and the effect was material, it is required to disclose the nature of the change and quantify its effect. That quantification is the single most valuable number in this whole exercise, because it converts a policy sentence into a dollar amount and tells you exactly how much of the year's reported result came from a decision rather than from operations. Search the document for the words useful life, estimate, and depreciation, and read every hit.

[P6] Fourth stop is the risk factors, which people skip because they are long and defensively written. Read the ones about technological change and obsolescence. What you are looking for is a mismatch between what the company tells investors about how fast its industry moves and what it has assumed in its depreciation schedule. A company that warns at length about rapid obsolescence in its risk factors, and assumes a long useful life in its policy note, has put two positions in one document that do not sit comfortably together. That tension is disclosed, it is legal, and it is the kind of thing a reader is expected to notice for themselves.

[P7] Fifth stop is the auditor's report, which sits just before the financial statements. Look for the section identifying matters that required especially difficult or subjective judgment. If asset useful lives appear there, the auditor is telling you, in the only register available to it, that this estimate was hard and that it received particular attention. That designation is not an adverse finding. It is a flag, and it is a deliberate one.

[P8] Sixth stop, if the company has meaningful debt, is the contractual obligations table and the debt note. You want the maturity profile, which tells you when principal is due, and any description of covenants. Covenant definitions are often not in the annual report at all but in the credit agreement, which is filed as an exhibit. Exhibits are public and almost never read. If the question you are asking is which covenants depend on the depreciation estimate, the exhibit is where the answer is, and it will be in the definitions section rather than the body.

[P9] Now, what to do about absence. Three things are frequently not disclosed, and their absence is itself a finding worth recording rather than an obstacle. Residual value assumptions are often simply not stated. Advance rates and the value curves a lender assumed are usually redacted from filed credit agreements as commercially sensitive. And the gross-to-net breakdown of a specific asset class within a broader category may not be separable. In each case the correct note to yourself is that the public record does not answer this, which is different from concluding that the answer is unfavourable.

[P10] One habit worth adopting, and it takes a few minutes. Before forming any view about a company's asset accounting, find the equivalent paragraph in the accounts of two comparable companies. Not the largest firms in the sector, which often have different economics, but the closest operating analogues. If they all say six years, the number is a convention and the interesting question is whether the convention is right. If they say four, five, and six, the number is a choice, and the interesting question is why this company chose the end of the range that it did.
