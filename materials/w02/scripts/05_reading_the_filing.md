## Script

[P1] Here are two sentences from two annual reports. The first says: property and equipment is stated at cost less accumulated depreciation, computed on a straight line basis over estimated useful lives of three to forty years. The second says: property and equipment is stated at cost less accumulated depreciation, computed on a straight line basis over estimated useful lives of buildings, thirty to forty years; leasehold improvements, the shorter of the lease term or fifteen years; production equipment, eight years; and information technology equipment, four years.

[P2] Both of those are compliant. Both were audited. The first one has told you almost nothing, because a range of three to forty years spans two orders of magnitude and does not tell you which end the company's actual revenue-generating assets sit at. The second has told you what you need. And the difference between them is a choice about disclosure granularity that the company made, and that you are entitled to notice.

[P3] Now, the assumption I want to break in this segment. People think the important numbers are in the financial statements: the income statement, the balance sheet, the cash flow. Those are the summary. Almost everything that matters for what we have been discussing is somewhere else. It is in the notes, in the management discussion, in the auditor's report, and in the exhibits, and the further from the front of the document you go, the less competition you have for the information.

[P4] So here is the route, in order. Seven stops, and it takes about twenty minutes once you know where you are going.

[P5] **First, the summary of significant accounting policies.** Almost always the first or second note. Find the paragraph headed property and equipment. It is short, usually four or five sentences, and it tells you three things: the measurement basis, which will say cost less accumulated depreciation; the method, which will say straight line unless something unusual is happening; and the useful lives, given as a range or as a list by category. That paragraph is the policy, and everything in the first two segments of this module is an elaboration of it.

[P6] Read the categories rather than the headline range, for the reason the two examples showed. What you want is the specific life assigned to the category that holds the assets which actually generate the revenue. If the disclosure is too coarse to tell you that, note the coarseness itself, because a company with one dominant asset class and a wide undifferentiated range has told you less than it appears to have told you.

[P7] **Second, the property and equipment note.** A table further into the notes, giving gross cost by category, accumulated depreciation, and the net carrying value that appears on the balance sheet. This table is more useful than it looks, because the ratio between the first two numbers tells you the age of the asset base. A company whose accumulated depreciation is ten per cent of gross cost has a young fleet and a long runway before replacement. One where it is seventy per cent has an old fleet and a large capital bill approaching. The same net carrying value can sit on either situation, and the balance sheet alone will not distinguish them.

[P8] **Third, management's discussion and analysis,** and specifically anywhere it mentions a change in estimate. If the company revised a useful life during the period and the effect was material, it is required to disclose the nature of the change and quantify the effect. That quantification is the single most valuable number in this exercise, because it converts a policy sentence into a dollar amount and tells you how much of the year's result came from a decision rather than from operations. Search the document for the words useful life, estimate, and depreciation, and read every hit.

[P9] **Fourth, the risk factors.** People skip these because they are long and defensively drafted, and that is a mistake for one narrow purpose. Read the ones about technological change and obsolescence, and compare them against the policy note. A company that warns at length about how fast its industry moves, and assumes a long useful life, has put two positions in one document that do not sit comfortably together. That tension is disclosed, it is entirely legal, and it is the kind of thing a reader is expected to notice for themselves.

[P10] **Fifth, the auditor's report,** which sits just before the financial statements and is two or three pages long. Look for the section identifying matters that required especially difficult or subjective judgment. If asset useful lives appear there, the auditor is telling you, in the only register available to it, that this estimate was hard and received particular attention. That is not an adverse finding. It is a flag, and it is deliberate, and the auditor chose to put it there.

[P11] **Sixth, if the company has meaningful debt, the credit agreements.** They are filed as exhibits, they are public, and almost nobody opens them. The annual report will describe covenants in general terms. The agreement contains the actual definitions, and as the last segment showed, the definitions are where the answer lives. Whether asset cover is tested against carried value or appraised value is one word in a definitions section, and it determines whether the company is marking its own homework.

[P12] **Seventh, and it is the cheapest check in this whole module: last year's filing.** Open the prior annual report and put the two policy notes side by side. You are looking for a sentence that has quietly changed. A useful life that was four to twenty and is now four to twenty-five. A category that has been merged into another. A residual value that used to be mentioned and no longer is. Companies must disclose a material change in estimate and they do, but the disclosure is a paragraph in the management discussion, and the policy note is where the change actually shows. Comparing two documents takes five minutes and catches things that reading one document carefully will not.

[P13] The same trick works across a quarter. Interim filings carry an abbreviated version of the policy note, and a change made mid-year appears there first, usually with less commentary than it will get in the annual report. If you are following a company closely, the interim filing is where a change surfaces earliest.

[P14] Now, what to do about absence, because three things are frequently missing and their absence is a finding rather than an obstacle. Residual value assumptions are often simply not stated, and the standard reading is that the company depreciates to zero. Advance rates and the value curves a lender assumed are usually redacted from filed agreements as commercially sensitive. And the split of a specific asset class within a broader category may not be separable at all. In each case the correct note to yourself is that the public record does not answer this, which is a different conclusion from deciding that the answer is unfavourable.

[P15] There is one habit worth adopting and it takes about ten minutes. Before forming any view about a company's asset accounting, find the equivalent paragraph in the accounts of two comparable companies. Not the largest firms in the sector, which usually have different economics, but the closest operating analogues. If all three say the same thing, the number is a convention, and the interesting question becomes whether the convention is right. If they say three different things, the number is a choice, and the interesting question is why this company chose the end of the range that it did.

[P16] Now one for you. Here is a policy note from a data processing company. Property and equipment is stated at cost less accumulated depreciation. Depreciation is computed using the straight line method over the estimated useful lives of the assets, which range from three to thirty years. Stop the recording and write down three things that note does not tell you, and where in the filing you would go for each.

[P17] Here is what I would have written. It does not tell you which category the computing equipment sits in, so I would go to the property and equipment note for the category breakdown. It does not state a residual value, so I would read the note for any mention of salvage, and failing that record that the company is presumed to depreciate to zero. And it does not say whether the estimate has ever been revised, so I would search the management discussion for a change in estimate and check the prior year's annual report to see whether the same sentence appeared with different numbers. That last check is the one people forget, and comparing this year's policy note against last year's is the cheapest thing in this entire segment.

[P18] The last segment of this module puts all of it together. We have looked at how the number is chosen, how it is revised, whether market movements reach it, what happens when somebody lends against it, and where all of that is written down. Next we look at the situation those five things combine to produce, which is a company carrying assets at a value the market would not pay, with everybody able to see it and nobody required to do anything.
