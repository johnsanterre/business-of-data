Carrying value against market value

Segment 6 of 6 · How an asset is carried

The narration as written, 19 paragraphs. This is the source the audio was generated from.

1Two companies. Each carries a fleet of specialised equipment at four hundred million dollars. Each fleet would fetch about two hundred and sixty million if sold today. So each is carrying roughly a hundred and forty million more than the market would pay, and in both cases the gap is visible to anyone who compares the accounts against auction results.

2The first company operates in that condition for eleven years and is never troubled by it. It passes every impairment test, never sells anything, never trips a covenant, and eventually replaces the fleet in the ordinary course. Nobody outside the finance function ever thinks about the gap. The second company fails inside eighteen months, and when it does, the write-down is the headline.

3The difference is not the size of the gap, the industry, the quality of the management, or the accounting policy. Both companies did the same thing and both did it properly. The difference is that the second one was eventually forced to sell, and the first one never was.

4That is the entire subject of this segment, and it is the point the whole module has been building toward, so let me set out the mechanism carefully before I say what to do about it.

5Recall how the gap opens. Carrying value follows a schedule chosen at acquisition, from an estimate of useful life. Market value follows demand. Those are independent processes with no channel between them. In a stable market they drift together loosely and nobody notices. In a market where the asset loses value faster than the schedule assumed, the two separate, and the separation widens every year the schedule stays unchanged.

6Now recall what would force recognition, because the list is short and the shortness is the point. An impairment test failure, which requires undiscounted future cash flows to fall below carrying value, a deliberately forgiving comparison. A reclassification to held for sale, which the company controls the timing of. An actual sale, which the company also controls. A revision to the useful life estimate, which the company controls entirely. Every trigger on that list is either high-barred or in management's hands. That is why the gap can sit there for a decade with nobody doing anything improper.

7Which produces a specific kind of fragility, and it has a recognisable shape. A company in this position is solvent on its own accounts and would not be if the assets were marked. It can operate indefinitely provided it never has to sell, never fails an impairment test, and never revises the estimate. Each of those conditions holds until it does not, and here is the part that matters: they tend to stop holding at the same time, because whatever forces a sale is usually the same thing that has depressed the market. That correlation is what turns a quiet accounting position into an event.

8There is a second-order effect that is easy to miss and that segment four set up. The gap is not confined to the accounts, because carrying values feed covenants and borrowing bases. A company carrying assets above market has borrowed against a number that overstates its cushion, and its lender's protection is thinner than the loan agreement makes it look. Frequently the lender knows and has priced it, through a lower advance rate or a higher coupon. Where the lender has not priced it, the exposure has moved from the borrower's accounts to the lender's balance sheet without either party recording the transfer.

9So what can a reader actually do about this from outside? Three things, and none of them requires inside information.

10First, compare the useful life against the closest operating comparables, and treat a company sitting at the long end of the range as making a claim that requires evidence rather than exercising a default. Second, look for a market signal about the assets themselves: resale prices, rental or lease rates, auction results, the prices at which comparable assets have actually changed hands. That evidence often exists in trade sources even when it is entirely absent from the filings.

11Third, and this is the one I want you to take away, calculate what the accounts would look like under a shorter life. It is arithmetic, it takes ten minutes, and it requires nothing but disclosed numbers. Take the gross cost of the asset base from the property and equipment note. Apply a shorter life, one you can defend from the comparables or from the market evidence. Compute the depreciation charge that would result. Substitute it for the reported one, and look at what happens to operating income.

12Let me do one with you so the mechanics are concrete. A company's property and equipment note shows gross cost of two billion four hundred million, and the policy note says a six year life on the category that matters. Straight line over six years is four hundred million a year of depreciation, and the income statement confirms roughly that. The company reports operating income of one hundred and twenty million.

13Now substitute a four year life, which the comparables support. Two billion four hundred million over four years is six hundred million a year. That is two hundred million more depreciation than reported. Take that off the one hundred and twenty million of operating income, and the company reports an eighty million dollar operating loss instead of a profit. The cash is identical in both versions. Every customer, every contract, every invoice is identical. The difference between a profitable company and a loss-making one, on those numbers, is two years of assumed life.

14That test has two outcomes and both are informative. If the company remains profitable under the shorter life, then the estimate is not carrying much weight, and you can stop worrying about it and go and look at something else. If reported profit disappears, then the company's profitability is a function of an assumption rather than of operations, and every other question you ask about that company should be asked with that in mind. It does not tell you the company is wrong. It tells you how much rides on its being right.

15Now I want to close by breaking one more assumption, and it is the one most of you have been quietly forming for the last ninety minutes. By this point in the module the natural conclusion is that this is all a bit of a racket. Management picks the number, management benefits from the number, the auditor can only say the number is reasonable, the gap goes unrecognised for years, and the whole thing collapses at the worst moment. It looks like a system designed to hide things.

16Treating it that way will make you a worse analyst, and I want to say why. Historical cost accounting is a considered choice with real advantages, and the chief one is that it is verifiable and very hard to manipulate compared with the alternative. The alternative is asking managers to tell you every quarter what they think their assets are worth, which puts an unfalsifiable estimate at the centre of the accounts instead of at the edge of them. Every system for measuring anything trades one kind of error for another, and this one trades currency for verifiability, deliberately, with the cost known and accepted.

17The estimates inside it are made in good faith far more often than not. Most companies that carry assets above market are not concealing anything; they made a reasonable judgment some years ago and the world moved. The point is not that the numbers are false. The point is that they are the output of a process with known properties, and once you know the properties you know which questions the numbers can answer and which they cannot.

18So here is what to carry out of this module. The accounts can tell you what a company spent and how it has decided to allocate that spending across time. They are good at that, and they are auditable at that. They cannot tell you what the assets would fetch today, and they are not designed to. When those two questions happen to have the same answer, the accounts are a fine guide to the company's position. When they diverge, the accounts remain entirely correct and stop being sufficient. The difference between a competent reader and an incompetent one is simply knowing which of those two situations you are in, and the way you find out is to go and look.
