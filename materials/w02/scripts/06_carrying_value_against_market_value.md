## Script

[P1] This module has been building toward one situation, and this segment names it. A company owns assets carried at a value the market would not pay. Everybody involved can see it. The company can see it, the auditor can see it, the lenders can see it, and any reader who does the arithmetic can see it. And nothing in the accounting system requires anyone to write it down. That is not a scandal and it is not a loophole. It is the predictable output of a system built on historical cost, and it is worth understanding as a mechanism rather than as a misdeed.

[P2] Recall how the gap opens. Carrying value follows a schedule chosen at acquisition, based on an estimate of useful life. Market value follows demand. Those two are independent processes. In a stable market they track each other loosely and nobody notices. In a market where the asset loses value faster than the schedule assumes, the two curves separate, and the separation widens every year that the schedule stays unchanged.

[P3] Now recall what would force recognition, because the list is short. An impairment test failure, which requires that the asset's undiscounted future cash flows fall below its carrying amount, a deliberately forgiving comparison. A reclassification to held for sale, which the company controls the timing of. An actual sale, which the company also controls. A change in the useful life estimate, which the company controls entirely. Every one of those triggers is either high-barred or in management's hands, which is why the gap can persist for years without anybody doing anything improper.

[P4] The consequence is a specific kind of fragility, and it has a shape worth recognising. A company in this position is solvent on its own accounts and would not be if the assets were marked. It can operate indefinitely as long as it never has to sell, never trips an impairment test, and never revises its estimate. Each of those conditions holds until it does not, and they tend to stop holding at the same time, because the event that forces a sale is usually the same event that has depressed the market.

[P5] There is a second-order effect that is easy to miss. The gap is not only an accounting issue, because carrying values feed covenants and borrowing bases. So a company carrying assets above market has borrowed against a number that overstates its cushion, and its lender's protection is thinner than the loan agreement makes it look. The lender frequently knows this and has priced it, through a lower advance rate or a higher coupon. When the lender has not priced it, the gap has been transferred from the borrower's accounts to the lender's balance sheet without either party recording the transfer.

[P6] What can a reader actually do about this from outside? Three things, none of which require inside information. First, compare the useful life against the closest operating comparables, and treat a company at the long end of the range as making a claim that needs evidence rather than a default choice. Second, look for any market signal about the assets themselves: resale prices, rental or lease rates, auction results, the prices at which comparable assets have changed hands. That evidence often exists in trade sources even when it is absent from the filings. Third, calculate what the accounts would look like under a shorter life, which is arithmetic and takes ten minutes, and see whether the company's reported profitability survives it.

[P7] That third exercise deserves emphasis, because it is the most useful single test in this module and it requires nothing but the disclosed numbers. Take gross cost of the asset base, apply a shorter life, compute the depreciation charge that would result, and substitute it for the reported one. If the company remains profitable, the estimate is not doing much work and you can stop worrying about it. If reported profit disappears, then the company's profitability is a function of an assumption rather than of operations, and every other question you ask about that company should be asked with this in mind.

[P8] A closing word on how to hold this. Nothing described in this module is fraud, and treating it as fraud will make you a worse analyst. Historical cost accounting is a considered choice with real advantages, chief among them that it is verifiable and hard to manipulate compared with the alternative, which is asking managers to tell you what they think their assets are worth. Estimates within it are made in good faith far more often than not. The point is not that the numbers are false. The point is that they are the output of a process with known properties, and once you know the properties you know which questions the numbers can answer and which they cannot.

[P9] What they can answer is what the company spent and how it has decided to allocate that spending across time. What they cannot answer, and are not designed to answer, is what the assets would fetch today. When those two questions have the same answer, the accounts are a good guide to the company's position. When they diverge, the accounts remain correct and stop being sufficient, and the difference between a competent reader and an incompetent one is knowing which of those two situations you are in.
