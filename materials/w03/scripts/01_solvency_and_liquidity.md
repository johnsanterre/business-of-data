## Script

[P1] I am going to describe two lenders and withhold the explanation for a while. Harbor Savings and Meridian Trust both lend against commercial property in the same country, in the same years, under the same regulator. At the start of a rising rate cycle their balance sheets are, to two significant figures, the same balance sheet. Each holds a loan book of nine hundred million dollars. Each owes eight hundred million to the people who funded it. Each therefore reports one hundred million dollars of equity, which is the cushion between what it owns and what it owes. If you put the two summary pages side by side on a desk, you would not be able to tell which was which.

[P2] Two years into the rate cycle, one of these lenders has been closed by its regulator and the other is still open and still lending. I want you to guess which, and I want you to notice what you used to guess. Almost everyone reaches for the same thing, which is the quality of the loan book, and asks which one made the worse loans. That is a reasonable place to look. It is also, in this case, going to send you to the wrong answer, because the lender that failed is the one whose loans were fine.

[P3] Here are the numbers at the moment of the failure. Harbor, the survivor, is holding a loan book that is realistically worth about seven hundred and forty million dollars against eight hundred million of obligations. Harbor's cushion is not one hundred million any more. It is negative sixty million. Harbor owes sixty million dollars more than everything it owns is worth. Meridian, the one that was closed, is holding a book worth about nine hundred and forty million against the same eight hundred million of obligations, so Meridian's cushion had grown from one hundred million to one hundred and forty. The lender with a negative cushion is open. The lender with the larger positive cushion is shut.

[P4] Let me say out loud the thing most people conclude when they first meet this, because naming it precisely is most of the work. The natural reading is that a business fails when it runs out of value, that the balance sheet is the scoreboard, and that a firm with one hundred and forty million dollars of equity has one hundred and forty million dollars of room before anything bad can happen to it. That reading is not sloppy. It is what the word equity is for, and it is correct about something. It is simply not correct about failure, because failure is not an event that happens on a balance sheet.

[P5] Failure happens on a calendar. A firm fails when a specific amount of money is due to a specific party on a specific day and the firm does not have it. That is the whole definition. Notice what is absent from it. There is no reference to what the firm owns, no reference to what the firm is worth, and no reference to next year. A payment was due on Thursday and the money was not there on Thursday. Everything else in this segment is downstream of that sentence, so it is worth sitting with the fact that the operative test has a date in it and the balance sheet does not.

[P6] So there are two different questions being asked about a firm, and it is worth separating them carefully because in ordinary speech they get the same word. The first question is solvency. Solvency asks whether everything the firm owns is worth more than everything the firm owes, with no deadline attached to the question at all. It is answered by a valuation, it covers the whole balance sheet, and it is fundamentally an opinion about the future, because most of what a lender owns is a promise that somebody else will pay later. Harbor fails this test. It is insolvent.

[P7] The second question is liquidity. Liquidity asks whether the cash available on a particular day is at least the cash demanded on that particular day. It is answered by a calendar rather than a valuation, it covers only the near term, and it is a fact rather than an opinion, because by close of business either the payment cleared or it did not. Meridian fails this test. It is illiquid. A firm can fail either test independently of the other, and the two failures have different causes, different cures, and speeds that differ by orders of magnitude.

[P8] Take Harbor first, and be clear about how bad its position genuinely is. Harbor lent against offices. Two years of rising rates and falling occupancy have left a large part of that book worth well under its face value, and if Harbor were required today to mark every loan to what somebody would actually pay for it, the write-down would be about one hundred and sixty million dollars against a cushion of one hundred. Harbor is not marginally impaired. It is comprehensively insolvent, and its own management knows this.

[P9] And yet nobody can do anything about it, because of how Harbor is funded. Harbor's eight hundred million was not borrowed from depositors. It was raised by selling ten-year bonds to three insurance companies, and those bonds do not mature for another eight years. There is no clause that lets an insurer demand its money back early because it has become worried. So the only cash Harbor must find is the semi-annual coupon on those bonds, and enough of Harbor's borrowers are still paying their interest to cover it. Harbor is insolvent and it will remain open for years, because nobody can present a claim it cannot pay.

[P10] Now Meridian, which had the better year. Meridian lent against warehouses and grocery-anchored retail, both of which held their occupancy through the cycle. Every loan in the book is performing, not one borrower has missed a payment, and the book is worth a little more than its face value because the loans carry rates above what a new borrower could get today. Meridian's cushion is one hundred and forty million dollars and it is real. An auditor examining that balance sheet would sign it without hesitation, and would be right to.

[P11] Meridian's funding is the part nobody was looking at. That eight hundred million is deposits, held by roughly four hundred property developers, almost all of whom operate in the same metropolitan area. They bank with Meridian for a specific reason: Meridian lends to them, and keeping the operating account where the credit line is has always been the arrangement. So the deposit base was assembled by the lending business, which means the four hundred depositors are not four hundred independent opinions about Meridian. They are four hundred people in the same industry, in the same city, who talk to each other constantly.

[P12] On a Monday in the second year, a rumour begins that Meridian is in trouble. The rumour is false. It appears to have started from a misreading of a regulatory filing by a firm with no particular knowledge of Meridian. By Tuesday afternoon several developers have moved their operating balances as a precaution, which is individually a sensible thing to do and costs them almost nothing. By Thursday, two hundred and sixty million dollars has left the bank. Nothing about the loan book has changed in those four days. Every borrower is still paying. The rumour is still false.

[P13] Meridian holds forty million dollars in cash, so the first forty million of withdrawals is met without difficulty and then the cash is gone. To meet the remaining two hundred and twenty million, Meridian has to sell loans, and here is where the shape of the asset matters. These are ten-year property loans, individually negotiated, each with its own borrower and its own covenants. There is no exchange for them. Selling one requires finding a buyer who is willing to underwrite that specific borrower, and doing that in four days rather than four months means accepting whoever is available. The best bid Meridian can obtain on a block of them is seventy-eight cents on the dollar.

[P14] So the arithmetic of the rescue is this. To raise two hundred and twenty million dollars at seventy-eight cents, Meridian must sell two hundred and eighty-two million dollars of face value. The difference between what those loans were carried at and what they fetched is sixty-two million dollars, and that loss is now real and booked. Meridian's cushion falls from one hundred and forty million to seventy-eight million. Notice that Meridian is still solvent after the sale. It still owns more than it owes. It has simply paid sixty-two million dollars for the privilege of turning its own good assets into cash quickly.

[P15] Then the sale becomes known, and this is the part that closes the bank. The buyer of a two hundred and eighty-two million dollar block of loans is not a secret, and within a day the remaining depositors learn that Meridian sold assets at seventy-eight cents to meet withdrawals. Up to that moment a depositor could reasonably believe the rumour was nonsense. Now there is a fact in the world that looks exactly like what a failing bank does. The remaining five hundred and forty million of deposits begins to move, and it moves faster than the first wave, because now the depositors are not reacting to a rumour but to each other.

[P16] Meridian's second sale is worse than the first, because everyone selling into a market knows the seller is forced. On day eleven the regulator closes the bank. The final accounting shows that Meridian's borrowers, over the whole episode, missed nothing. The loans were good. The bank died holding good loans, having been solvent on the morning it opened for the last time.

[P17] The mechanism that killed it is worth naming, because it is the reason these two failures cannot be kept fully separate. A firm that is illiquid but solvent has one obvious remedy available, which is to sell something. But selling under time pressure means selling at a discount, and the discount is a real loss that reduces the cushion. Sell enough, fast enough, and a solvent firm becomes an insolvent one, by its own hand, using the only tool it had. The forced sale is the bridge between the two failures, and it runs in one direction only. Illiquidity can manufacture insolvency in a fortnight. Insolvency, as Harbor demonstrates, can sit there for years without manufacturing anything.

[P18] I want to look at the same failure from four positions, because each one sees a different part of it and none of them sees all of it. Start with the treasurer, who is the person in the building whose job this actually is. A treasurer does not primarily work from a balance sheet. A treasurer works from a cash calendar, which is a forward schedule of money coming in and money going out, day by day for the near dates and week by week further out. The question that governs the treasurer's morning is not what the firm is worth. It is what is due on Thursday and where Thursday's money is coming from.

[P19] Second position, the auditor. An auditor's report contains a going-concern assessment, which sounds like an accounting judgment and is for the most part a liquidity judgment wearing accounting clothes. The auditor is asking whether the firm can meet its obligations for the next twelve months, which requires a view on whether funding will still be there. That is why a going-concern qualification is treated as such a severe signal: it is one of the few places where the liquidity question is forced into a public document, and it is answered by a party with a professional obligation to say so.

[P20] Third position, a secured lender to the firm. This party watches the collateral, and collateral is a solvency concept, so the lender's covenants are written in solvency language about asset values and coverage ratios. But when the lender actually withdraws the line, the trigger is usually not a covenant breach. It is the fear of one, or the sight of other creditors moving first. So this party thinks in solvency and acts on liquidity, and the gap between those two things is where a great deal of damage happens, because the firm has been managing to the covenant and the line disappears before the covenant is anywhere near breached.

[P21] Fourth position, the depositor. This party has read no accounts, holds no view on the loan book, and could not tell you the firm's equity to the nearest hundred million. The depositor is responding to two things only: what it costs to move the money, which is nearly nothing, and what everybody else appears to be doing. It is worth being precise about the incentive here, because it is not panic and it is not stupidity. If there is any chance others will withdraw, and the firm cannot pay everyone, then withdrawing early is simply the correct decision for each individual party. The behaviour that destroys the firm is rational at every desk.

[P22] Put the four positions together and the asymmetry in speed follows. Insolvency is cured slowly, by raising new capital, by earnings accumulating over years, or by creditors agreeing to take less. Every one of those cures takes months at minimum, and Harbor shows that a firm can wait. Illiquidity is cured in hours, by a credit line, by a central bank, or by selling assets, and if none of the three is available on the day the money is due, there is no fourth option and no adjournment. The slower failure is survivable precisely because it is slow. The faster one is fatal precisely because it is fast.

[P23] Let me give you a firm to diagnose. Coastwise Leasing owns containers and leases them to shipping lines. Its containers would fetch six hundred and twenty million dollars in an orderly sale over about a year. It owes five hundred and eighty million dollars, of which forty million is a bank facility that matures in six weeks. Its leases generate about four million dollars a month. It holds nine million dollars in cash. The bank has told Coastwise it does not intend to renew the facility. Work out which test Coastwise passes and which it fails before I answer.

[P24] Coastwise passes the solvency test comfortably. Six hundred and twenty million against five hundred and eighty is a cushion of forty million dollars, and nothing about the containers is impaired. Coastwise fails the liquidity test. In six weeks it must produce forty million dollars. It will have nine million of cash plus roughly six million of lease receipts in that window, which is fifteen million, so it is twenty-five million short on a date that is already fixed. Its assets are containers, which take about a year to sell properly, and the one asset sale it could do quickly would be at a discount that eats most of the forty million cushion. Coastwise is solvent and going to fail, and it will fail in six weeks unless somebody refinances that facility.

[P25] Now a second one, with less given to you. Pallant Industrial has never missed a payment, has no debt maturing for four years, and reports negative equity of ninety million dollars after writing down a factory. Its operations produce eleven million dollars of cash a month and its interest bill is four million a month. Ask yourself which test it fails, whether it is going to fail this year, and what single fact you would want that I have not told you. The answer is that Pallant fails the solvency test and passes the liquidity test with room to spare, so it will not fail this year and probably not for several. The fact worth wanting is whether any of its agreements contain a covenant tied to net worth, because negative equity of ninety million is exactly the condition such a covenant tests, and a breach would convert a comfortable four-year maturity into a demand for immediate repayment. That is the mechanism by which a solvency problem is converted into a liquidity problem, and it is written into the documents in advance.

[P26] The uncomfortable part of this segment is the part I would ask you to hold onto rather than resolve. Meridian's accounts said it had one hundred and forty million dollars of equity, and the accounts were right. The auditor who signed them was right. The regulator who reviewed the loan book was right. Every number in the public record was accurate and the institution was gone in eleven days, and no amount of care taken over those numbers would have prevented it, because the numbers were answering the solvency question and the firm died of the other one. The instinct to treat a strong balance sheet as reassurance is not wrong so much as incomplete, and the missing half is not in the accounts at all.

[P27] What the missing half consists of is the subject of the rest of this module. The next segment takes up duration, which is the mismatch between how long the firm's funding lasts and how long its assets take to return cash, and it is the structural condition that made Meridian vulnerable and Harbor safe. After that we look at the runway calculation, which is the same arithmetic applied to a firm with no revenue yet, and then at concentration, which is why four hundred depositors behaved as one.
