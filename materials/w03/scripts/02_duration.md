## Script

[P1] I want to start with two firms holding the same asset, and I mean that more strictly than it usually gets meant. Ridgeline Capital and Calder Partners each hold one hundred million dollars of a single commercial property loan. Not two similar loans to two similar borrowers. One loan, to one borrower, on one building, syndicated between them in equal halves. Ten years to run, five per cent paid annually, principal at the end. Whatever happens to that building happens to both of them identically and at the same instant, and there is no possible difference between them in credit judgment, underwriting, or luck.

[P2] Rates rise by two percentage points over the following year. At the end of that year Ridgeline files a set of accounts in which almost nothing has happened. Calder is recapitalised by its owners in an emergency over a weekend, and the alternative on the table was closure. Same borrower, same building, same loan, same rate move, same year. One firm barely notices and the other nearly dies.

[P3] Before going further I want to close off the obvious escape routes, because the interesting part of this example is how few there are. The borrower did not miss a payment. The building did not lose a tenant. Neither firm sold anything voluntarily, neither made a second investment that went wrong, and neither had a fraud. The only difference between Ridgeline and Calder is on the other side of the balance sheet, in where each of them got the hundred million dollars it lent.

[P4] Let me state the belief that stands between most people and this material, because it is not a naive belief and it is held by people who do this professionally. The belief is that a change in the market price of a loan you intend to hold until it matures is a paper loss, that it will pull back to par as the loan approaches maturity, and that it therefore does not represent anything real. Somebody holding that view will say, entirely sincerely, that they will collect one hundred cents on the dollar in year ten regardless of what a screen says in year two.

[P5] And that is true. If you hold the loan to maturity and the borrower pays, you collect par, and the intervening price moves net to nothing. The problem is the word if, and specifically that the ability to hold to maturity is not a decision you get to make. It is a property of your funding. You can hold an asset for ten years only if nobody can demand your money back inside ten years. Whether that is so is written in your liabilities, not in your intentions, and Calder's intentions were identical to Ridgeline's.

[P6] To see the difference you need a dimension that a balance sheet does not have. A balance sheet is an instant. It lists what is owned and what is owed at one moment, and it has no time axis at all. But every item on both sides of it has dates attached. The loan returns cash on a schedule stretching over ten years. The funding must be repaid on its own schedule. The balance sheet displays the amounts and suppresses the schedules, and the entire subject of this segment lives in the schedules.

[P7] The measurement that puts the time axis back is duration. The plain-language version is this: duration is the average length of time you wait to get your money, where each payment is weighted by how much of the total value it represents. A loan that returns everything in one lump at year ten has a duration of ten years. A loan that pays you back steadily has a shorter duration, because much of your money comes home early even though the final payment is still at year ten.

[P8] For our loan the number is about eight point one years. It is less than ten because the annual coupons arrive before maturity and pull the average down, and it is close to ten because those coupons are small relative to the principal, so most of the weight still sits at the end. That is worth holding onto as a rule of thumb: for a loan that pays interest only and returns principal at maturity, duration is a bit less than the term, and the lower the coupon the closer duration gets to the term itself.

[P9] The reason anyone computes this is not curiosity about averages. Duration is the sensitivity of the asset's price to a change in rates, and it is used as a multiplier. Adjusted slightly for the level of rates, our loan has a modified duration of about seven point seven, and what that number means operationally is that a one percentage point rise in rates should cut the price of the loan by about seven point seven per cent. It converts a rate move into a price move, which is the conversion the firm actually needs.

[P10] So let us check it against the exact arithmetic rather than trusting it. Rates rise two points, from five per cent to seven. The duration estimate says the price should fall by about fifteen point four per cent. The exact present value calculation says the price falls to eighty-five point nine five, which is a fall of fourteen point zero per cent. The estimate is in the right region and it is wrong by one and a half points, and it is wrong in a specific direction that repeats.

[P11] Push the shock to three points and the pattern is clearer. Duration predicts a fall of twenty-three point two per cent. The exact price is seventy-nine point nine, a fall of twenty point one per cent. Duration has again overstated the damage, and it has overstated it by more. That systematic error has a name, convexity, and its practical content is a piece of good news: for a plain loan, duration exaggerates the loss when rates rise and understates the gain when they fall. The error is in the conservative direction, and it grows with the size of the shock, which means duration is a reliable early warning and an unreliable precise forecast.

[P12] Now turn to the liabilities, where the same measurement applies and produces a number that looks like a typographical error. A deposit repayable on demand must be returned tomorrow if the depositor asks tomorrow. Its duration is about one day, which is roughly three thousandths of a year. A ten-year bond sold to an insurance company has a duration of several years. Term funding from a bank, three years with no early repayment right, has a duration of about three. The liability side has a duration just as the asset side does, and nobody has to compute it to a decimal place to see that these are different worlds.

[P13] Except that no bank actually uses one day, and the reason is sound. Contractually a deposit can leave tomorrow. Behaviourally, most deposits sit for years: people do not move their current account because a rate moved, and the balance in aggregate is far stickier than any individual account. So a bank models a behavioural duration instead of a contractual one, and assigns its deposit base a life of three or four years on the evidence of how the balances have actually behaved. That is not a dodge. Using one day would overstate the risk so severely that the firm could not operate.

[P14] The parameter that carries this is called deposit beta, and it is the fraction of a rate rise the bank has to pass on in order to keep the money. If beta is three tenths, then a two point rise in market rates costs the bank six tenths of a point in funding, not two points, and the deposits behave like a long-dated liability at a below-market rate. Estimate a beta of three tenths and a four year behavioural life, and the measured duration gap on our book falls from eight point one years to about four. The firm has not changed anything. It has measured the same position with a better model and got half the number.

[P15] Now the difficulty, which is not that the model is wrong but that of everything in this segment it is the only quantity estimated from history rather than read off a contract. The duration of the loan is arithmetic on a payment schedule that is written down. The behavioural life of a deposit is a regression on the past, and the past it is fitted to may contain no episode in which the depositor had a genuinely better alternative that was easy to reach. A beta estimated across a decade of near-zero rates is estimated on a period in which the parameter was never tested, because there was nothing to switch to.

[P16] It is worse than an ordinary estimation problem, because beta is not a constant that we happen to measure imprecisely. It is a function of the alternative available, and that function is close to flat and then close to vertical. While a money market fund pays a tenth of a per cent, almost nobody moves and beta sits near zero. When the fund pays four per cent and the bank pays half, the calculation becomes obvious to an ordinary person and beta goes to something near one, quickly, and for everybody at once because everybody is looking at the same two numbers.

[P17] So the measured duration gap is smallest precisely when it is least trustworthy. In the calm period the model says four years and the model is fitted to calm data. In the stressed period the true figure reverts toward the contractual one day, and it reverts at the same moment the assets are worth least. A firm that fails this way had a number in a monthly report saying the gap was manageable. That number was produced in good faith, by a method that stops describing the world at the point the world starts mattering.

[P18] The quantity that matters is the difference between the two, and it is called the duration gap. Take the duration of the assets and subtract the duration of the liabilities. For a book of our ten-year loans funded by demand deposits, that is eight point one one minus about three thousandths, which is a gap of eight point one zero years. For the same book funded by ten-year bonds, the gap is close to zero. Two firms, one asset, and a difference of eight years in the only measurement that captures the thing that killed one of them.

[P19] It is worth translating the gap out of years and into an ordinary sentence, because as a number it slides past people. A duration gap of eight years means the firm has promised to return money tomorrow that it has lent out for eight years. That is the whole business. It is not a distortion of banking, it is banking, and it is genuinely valuable: somebody has to bridge the fact that savers want their money available and borrowers want it for a decade. The gap is the service being provided. It is also the exposure, and the firm is paid for carrying it.

[P20] So back to Ridgeline and Calder. Ridgeline funded its half of the loan by selling ten-year bonds to a pension fund. Its asset duration is eight point one and its liability duration is about nine, so its gap is slightly negative and effectively zero. When rates rose two points, the market value of Ridgeline's loan fell by fourteen per cent and the market value of Ridgeline's own debt fell by a similar proportion, because the same rate rise makes its bonds worth less to whoever holds them. The two moves offset. Ridgeline's accounts show a year in which nothing much happened, and that is not an accounting trick, it is an accurate description.

[P21] Calder funded its half with deposits. Its gap is eight point one years. When rates rose, the value of its loan fell by fourteen per cent and the value of its liabilities did not fall at all, because a dollar owed on demand is worth a dollar no matter what rates do. There was no offset available. The entire fourteen per cent came out of the only thing standing between the two sides, which is Calder's equity, and Calder's equity was nowhere near fourteen per cent of its assets.

[P22] Now I want to be precise about why a solvency test does not catch this, because it is not that anyone was careless. A solvency test compares assets to liabilities at an instant. Duration is a statement about how each side behaves across time. The test omits the axis on which the risk is entirely defined. Running the solvency test more often does not help, and running it more carefully does not help, because the quantity is not in the test's field of view at all. This is a case where a firm can be measured diligently by the wrong instrument, and diligence produces false comfort rather than no comfort, which is worse.

[P23] There is a second channel of damage running at the same time, and it hits the income statement rather than the balance sheet. Calder's loan pays five per cent, fixed, for ten years. Calder's deposits reprice immediately, because a depositor who can leave tomorrow will leave tomorrow unless paid the going rate. When rates rise two points, Calder's funding cost rises by roughly two points within weeks while its asset income does not move at all. The margin the business runs on is compressed, and it can go negative, at which point the firm loses money on every dollar it has lent.

[P24] So the same rate rise does two things to a firm with a duration gap, and it does them together. It reduces what the assets are worth, and it raises what the funding costs. A firm that had been comfortable on both measures can fail either the capital test or the earnings test, and typically starts failing both in the same quarter. These are not two risks that happen to arrive at once. They are one risk observed in two statements.

[P25] There is an accounting decision that determines whether any of this is visible, and it is the classification choice from the previous week's module. An asset a firm intends to hold to maturity may be carried at what it cost, with the fall in market value disclosed but not run through the accounts. An asset available for sale is carried at market. Two firms holding the identical loan with the identical loss will therefore report differently, and the one reporting nothing is not doing anything improper.

[P26] What makes that choice dangerous rather than merely permissive is the condition attached to it. A firm may use the hold-to-maturity treatment only while it has both the intent and the ability to hold, and selling from that portfolio can call the whole classification into question and force the rest of it to market. So the treatment works exactly until the day the firm needs cash, and on that day it stops working, and it stops working for the entire portfolio at once rather than for the piece being sold. The accounting reprices at the moment of maximum stress, which is the moment its concealment was doing the most work.

[P27] Let me put the numbers from the previous segment through this machinery, because the result changes how that failure should be read. Meridian's book was nine hundred and forty million dollars of exactly these loans, funded by eight hundred million of deposits, with a cushion of one hundred and forty million. Apply a two point rise in rates to that book. The market value falls to eight hundred and eight million. The liabilities are unchanged at eight hundred. The cushion is now eight million dollars.

[P28] Apply three points instead. The book is worth seven hundred and fifty point eight million against eight hundred million of liabilities, and the cushion is negative forty-nine point two million. Meridian, the lender whose every borrower paid on time and whose accounts an auditor signed without hesitation, is insolvent on a three point rate move with no credit event of any kind. Nothing has happened to a building. Nothing has happened to a borrower. A number moved on a screen and the equity was gone.

[P29] Which means the sentence I used in the previous segment was too generous. I said Meridian was solvent. It is more accurate to say Meridian was solvent at the rates prevailing that morning, and that its one hundred and forty million dollars of equity was not a stock of value the firm possessed but a reading taken at a particular point in a rate cycle. Nobody at Meridian had computed the duration gap, so nobody knew that the cushion had a rate attached to it, and the accounts had no place to say so.

[P30] Now the part that makes this a liquidity subject rather than an interest-rate subject. The rate rise that damages the asset is the same event that makes the funding leave. A depositor earning half a per cent watches the central bank raise rates two points, discovers that a money market fund now pays four, and moves. That depositor is not panicking and has heard no rumour. They are responding to price, sensibly, and the withdrawal they cause arrives precisely when the firm's assets are worth least and can only be sold at a discount.

[P31] So the trigger and the damage share a cause, and this is the structural point of the segment. If withdrawals arrived at random moments, a firm could hold a modest buffer and be fine, because sometimes it would be selling into a good market. Withdrawals do not arrive at random. They cluster at exactly the moment the assets are worth least, because one event produces both. That correlation is what makes the exposure dangerous rather than merely volatile, and it is why the forced sale from the previous segment is not bad luck but the expected outcome of an unhedged gap.

[P32] Let me look at this from inside the firm, from the committee whose job it is. An asset and liability committee exists to measure and manage this gap, and it meets monthly with a report in front of it showing the repricing profile of both sides. The instruments available to it are real: it can shorten the assets by lending at floating rates, lengthen the liabilities by issuing term debt or buying deposit stickiness, or hedge with an interest rate swap. So the tool exists, the committee exists, and the report exists, and firms with all three still fail this way.

[P33] They fail because closing the gap costs money in the near term and the cost is certain while the benefit is contingent. Term funding is more expensive than deposits. Floating rate loans earn a lower margin than fixed. A swap has a running cost and shows up as a loss in every quarter that rates do not move. So the committee that hedges reports worse earnings for several years and is asked why its competitors are more profitable, and the committee that does not hedge reports better earnings until the year it does not report anything. The incentive structure inside the firm is aligned against the measurement the firm is taking.

[P34] The outside view is the supervisory stress test, which exists because of exactly that incentive problem. A stress test does not ask what the firm is worth today. It applies a specified shock, two or three hundred basis points, and asks what the firm is worth afterwards. That is a way of forcing the time axis and the tail into a document, and it is why a firm can pass every capital ratio and fail a stress test. The ratios describe the present. The stress test asks a question about a state that has not happened.

[P35] Here is one to work through. Fenwick Bank holds one point two billion dollars of fifteen-year fixed rate mortgages yielding four and a half per cent, with a duration of about eleven years. It is funded by one point one billion of deposits, of which six hundred million are held by twelve corporate treasurers, and a hundred million of equity. Rates rise two points. Estimate what happens to the equity, and separately say what you think happens to the deposits, and which of the two you would raise first if you sat on its board.

[P36] The capital arithmetic goes like this. A duration of eleven against a two point move implies a fall of roughly twenty-two per cent before convexity, and convexity will pull that back to something nearer nineteen, so call it a loss in the region of two hundred and thirty million dollars on a one point two billion book. Against one hundred million of equity, the firm is insolvent on a market value basis by more than a hundred million, while reporting nothing at all if those mortgages are classified as held to maturity.

[P37] The funding half is worse and it is the half I would raise first. Six hundred million dollars of deposits sitting with twelve corporate treasurers is not a deposit base, it is twelve phone numbers, and a corporate treasurer is a professional whose actual job is to move money to the best available rate. They will reprice or leave within weeks of the rate rise, and they will do it as a group because they are all reading the same rate screen. Fenwick's capital problem is severe and slow. Its funding problem is severe and fast, and the fast one arrives first and forces the sale that turns the slow one into a realised loss.

[P38] The next segment takes the same arithmetic to a firm that has no loan book and no deposits, which is a business burning cash it raised in advance against revenue that has not arrived. The vocabulary changes completely and the structure does not: there is a stock of money, a rate at which it leaves, and a date on which it is gone, and the question is which of the assumptions behind that date breaks first.
