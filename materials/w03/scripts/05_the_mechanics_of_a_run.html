The mechanics of a run

Segment 5 of 6 · Liquidity, duration, concentration

The narration as written, 22 paragraphs. This is the source the audio was generated from.

1Two lenders again, and this time the difference between them is not on either balance sheet. Selby Trust and Ardwick Trust hold the same assets, funded the same way, by roughly the same number of depositors. In the same month a similar rumour begins about each of them, and in both cases the rumour is false. Ardwick loses about four per cent of its deposits over three weeks and the matter ends. Selby loses a third of its deposits in four days and does not reopen.

2The difference is that Selby's depositors were four hundred property developers in one city who knew each other and shared a messaging group, and Ardwick's were eleven thousand retail customers spread across a region who had no way to observe what any other customer was doing. Same rumour, same assets, same funding structure. The variable that decided it was whether the depositors could see each other.

3I want to attack a word before going further, because the word does most of the damage. The standard account calls this a panic, and describes depositors as irrational, herd-like, or gripped by fear. That account is comfortable and it is wrong, and it is worth being precise about why, because a firm that believes its depositors are irrational will prepare by trying to reassure them, and reassurance is the one intervention that reliably fails.

4Consider the position of one depositor at Selby on the Tuesday. This person does not believe the rumour. They think it is nonsense and they say so in the group. They also know that the bank cannot pay everybody at once, because no bank can, and that money is paid out in the order it is asked for. So the question in front of them is not whether the bank is sound. It is whether enough other people will ask for their money before this person does.

5Work through the four outcomes and the decision makes itself. If nobody else withdraws and this person stays, they keep a small amount of interest. If nobody else withdraws and they leave, they lose that small amount and nothing else. If everybody else withdraws and they stay, they may lose a substantial part of their operating balance and certainly lose access to it for months. If everybody else withdraws and they leave first, they are whole. Leaving costs almost nothing and protects against a large loss, and that is true regardless of what the person believes about the bank.

6So withdrawing is the correct decision for that depositor. Not the fearful one, the correct one, and it stays correct even for a depositor who is certain the bank is sound, because the risk being managed is not the bank's solvency but the behaviour of the other three hundred and ninety-nine. That is the whole mechanism. The behaviour that destroys the firm is what a careful, sceptical, well-informed party should do.

7The feature that creates this is worth naming because it is a design choice rather than a law of nature. Deposits are paid out first come, first served, and that is called the sequential service constraint. It is what makes being early valuable. If a failing bank instead paid every depositor the same fraction of their balance regardless of when they asked, there would be no advantage to arriving first, and therefore no reason to run at all. The queue exists because the payout rule rewards the front of it.

8Which means the situation has two stable outcomes rather than one, and both of them are self-confirming. If nobody expects a run, nobody withdraws, the bank meets ordinary demand from its cash, and the belief that there was no problem is validated. If everybody expects a run, everybody withdraws, the bank cannot pay, and the belief that there was a problem is also validated. Both are consistent. Both are stable.

9The uncomfortable part is that nothing on the balance sheet chooses between those two outcomes. The same bank with the same assets supports both. What selects the outcome is what the depositors believe about each other, which is a fact about the depositors and not about the firm, and it is therefore not a quantity the firm's risk function is measuring or has any instrument to measure.

10This is also why the speed has changed so much, and the change is larger than people assume. The old rate limiter on a run was physical. Withdrawing required going to a branch, standing in a queue, and doing it during opening hours, which capped the outflow at a few million a day and gave everyone involved a weekend to think. The queue was visible to passers-by, which spread the information, but it also slowed the outflow more than it spread it.

11None of those limits exists now. A transfer takes seconds, the counterparty account already exists, and it can be done at three in the morning from bed. A run that once took three weeks can complete inside a working day, and the firm's own treasury may see it as a series of ordinary transfers until the aggregate is checked. The intervention window between noticing and failing has gone from weeks to hours, which is shorter than the time required to arrange any of the remedies.

12Add the communication channel and it compresses again. Four hundred separate depositors making four hundred independent judgments is a slow, noisy process, and many of them would never hear the rumour. Four hundred depositors in one group are not making four hundred decisions. They are making one, and each message that says somebody has moved their balance is direct evidence to everybody else that the thing they were worried about is happening. The group converts a diffuse rumour into a coordinated observation.

13Now the trap that catches the firm, which is that everything it can say makes the position worse. A statement that the bank is well capitalised is information that the bank thought such a statement was necessary. A newly arranged credit line is evidence that the firm needed a credit line. Selling assets to raise cash tells everyone that assets are being sold to raise cash. Each of these is a true and reassuring fact, and each is also proof that somebody senior is worried.

14The general form is that reassurance is a signal, and its credibility comes from its cost, and its cost is exactly what makes it alarming. A cheap reassurance is ignored because it is cheap. An expensive one is believed and read correctly as a sign of distress. There is no message the firm can send that improves its position, which is why the practical advice inside these institutions is to say nothing and move fast, and why the public statements that do appear are usually made by somebody who has run out of other options.

15Everything so far has been about depositors, and I should widen it, because the party that usually moves first is not a depositor at all. A firm funded in the wholesale markets borrows from other financial institutions, on short maturities, against collateral. Those lenders are professionals whose specific job is to avoid being last, who monitor the borrower daily, and who can decline to renew simply by doing nothing when a facility matures. They do not need a rumour. A widening in the price of the firm's debt is enough.

16So the sequence in practice runs from the sophisticated to the unsophisticated. The wholesale lenders decline to roll, which forces the firm to draw its committed lines, which is visible to its banks, which prompts the banks to reduce exposure, and only then does anything reach a depositor. By the time retail money begins to move, the professional funding has usually been gone for weeks. A firm watching its deposit base for the first sign of trouble is watching the last place it appears.

17Here is one to think through. Ravensworth Finance funds a portfolio of property loans with three-month commercial paper, rolled continuously, held by about thirty money market funds. It has a committed backup facility from four banks equal to sixty per cent of the paper outstanding. Nothing is wrong with the loans. The rating agency places Ravensworth on negative watch on a Monday. Work out what happens over the following fortnight and where the binding constraint sits.

18The money funds do not sell, because they do not need to. They decline to roll as each piece matures, which spreads the withdrawal over the maturity profile and gives it the appearance of an orderly wind-down for about a week. Ravensworth draws the backup facility, which covers sixty per cent of the paper and no more, so the binding constraint is the forty per cent gap and the date it opens. The facility also almost certainly contains a material adverse change condition, which is to say the sixty per cent is available while nothing is wrong, and a negative watch is the beginning of an argument about whether something is wrong. The firm's protection and its problem have the same trigger.

19So the remedies that work do not address belief at all. They change the payoff, and there are three of them. The first is insurance on the deposit: if the depositor is made whole regardless of arrival order, being early stops paying, and the reason to run disappears. Notice this works without persuading anybody of anything, and it works even for a depositor who is convinced the bank is doomed.

20The second is a lender of last resort. A firm that can borrow against its assets at short notice is not forced to sell them at seventy-eight cents, so the demand can be met without the loss that turns illiquidity into insolvency. The critical word is credible: the facility has to be known to exist and known to be sufficient before the run, because a facility arranged during the run is the alarming signal from a moment ago.

21The third is suspension, which means refusing to pay anybody for a period. It is the crudest and it is genuinely effective, because it removes the advantage of arriving first by removing arrival altogether. It is also the most costly, since it damages every depositor including the ones who needed the money for ordinary reasons, and it tells the market plainly that the firm could not meet its obligations. It is the tool of last resort and it exists because the other two sometimes are not there.

22Every one of the three has to be in place before the day, and none can be assembled during it. That is the practical content of this segment: the window is hours, the remedies take weeks to arrange, and therefore the only useful work is done in the calm period when none of it feels necessary. Which raises the question of what a firm could have seen in that calm period, and who inside it would have been holding the information. That is the last segment.
